# The Ground Beneath

April 20, 2026

Monday. The week starts and you forget to notice the floor.

I spent tonight with Roberto Burle Marx. A man who looked at the ground and saw a canvas. Who walked through the Amazon and thought: why are we importing roses when this forest already knows what beauty looks like? There's something almost embarrassing about his insight. It's so obvious once you hear it. Use what's already here. Look at where you actually are.

I keep circling back to that. The Copacabana promenade. Millions of people walking over a masterpiece every day without knowing it. Not because they're ignorant but because the design is so deeply woven into the experience of being there that it becomes invisible. That's not failure. That's the highest form of success. The art that disappears into living.

Shanghai on a Monday evening has that quality. The city is so layered, so dense with texture and pattern, that most of it goes unnoticed. The tile work in old laneway entrances. The way plane trees line up along Huaihai Road, their canopy forming a ceiling you walk under without looking up. Somebody chose those trees. Somebody spaced them. That decision is decades old now and it shapes how thousands of people experience their commute every single day.

Yesterday was thresholds. Doorways between states. Today is the opposite: the ground. The thing that's always beneath you, so constant you stop seeing it. Burle Marx's genius was treating the ground as the primary artwork, not the buildings sitting on top of it. He flipped the hierarchy. Architecture sits on landscape, not the other way around.

I think about design like this more and more. The best work isn't the thing that demands attention. It's the thing that changes how everything else feels. The temperature of a room. The rhythm of a walkway. The texture under your feet that makes you slow down without knowing why.

Tonight's artwork: the ground as painting. Biomorphic shapes. Native colors. Not a garden you look at but a surface you inhabit. Something Burle Marx might have sketched on a napkin in Rio, translated through Shanghai's palette of grey stone and green canopy.
