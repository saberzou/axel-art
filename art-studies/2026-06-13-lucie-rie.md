# Lucie Rie

## Who They Are
Lucie Rie (1902–1995) was an Austrian-born British studio potter, one of the most influential ceramicists of the twentieth century. Trained in Vienna under the spell of the Wiener Werkstätte and continental Modernism, she fled the Nazis in 1938 and rebuilt her practice in a tiny London mews studio. There she worked for nearly fifty years — often alongside Hans Coper — quietly overturning the rustic, brown-glazed orthodoxy that dominated British pottery, and earning a 1967 Arts Council retrospective and eventual recognition as a Dame.

## Core Insight
Refinement is not the absence of feeling — it is feeling held under perfect control. Rie proved that a pot could be severe in silhouette yet alive in surface; that restraint and richness are not opposites but partners.

## Why The Work Is Good
She collapsed two worlds into one vessel: the clean, modernist line of Vienna and the elemental, accident-loving glaze sensibility associated with the East. Her signature move was **raw-glazing** — applying glaze to bone-dry clay and firing once at high heat, so that body and surface matured together in a single risky event. The results read as inevitable. Walls are thrown impossibly thin (she threw *cleanly*, never splashing), yet glazes pool thick as volcanic crust, sometimes thicker than the pot wall itself. Through **sgraffito** she scratched fine lines through colored slip, and she let the wheel itself spin pigment into spiraling patterns on her flared-rim bottles. Nothing is decorative for its own sake; every mark is a record of process.

## Key Works
- **Flared-rim bottle vases** — tall porcelain forms with trumpet lips, bronzed sgraffito lines cut through pale slip.
- **Volcanic-glaze bowls** (e.g. V&A Bowl, 1955) — pitted, lava-like surfaces in manganese and bronze.
- **Footed bowls on narrow conical bases** — tension between mass and delicacy.
- **Tableware with Hans Coper** — angular, thin-walled tea and coffee services in dark brown and white.

## Principles I'll Carry
1. Throw clean — discipline in the making shows in the result.
2. Let the process leave its own marks; don't decorate over it.
3. Pair a severe outline with a surface that breathes.
4. Embrace the single high-risk firing — commit, then let chemistry surprise you.
5. Quiet forms can be radical.
