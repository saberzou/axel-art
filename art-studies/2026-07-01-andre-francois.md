# André François — Study (2026-07-01)

## Who They Are
André François (1915–2005) was a Romanian-born French illustrator, cartoonist, painter, and poster designer — one of the most quietly influential graphic artists of the 20th century. Born in Timișoara, he studied at the Budapest Academy, then moved to Paris in 1934 to apprentice under the legendary poster master A.M. Cassandre. He became a French citizen in 1939. Over six decades he produced 57 New Yorker covers, posters for Kodak, Citroën, and Gillette, children's books, theatre and ballet designs, and cartoons for Punch, Lilliput, Vogue, Holiday, and Fortune.

## Core Insight
**Wit is a drawing tool, not a caption.** François didn't illustrate a joke and then draw it — the humor *is* the line itself. His genius was making the visual idea and the comic idea inseparable, so the paradox lands before you've consciously "read" anything. He paired a deliberately rough, almost childlike hand with razor-sharp conceptual control: the scribble looks innocent, but the thinking underneath is merciless.

## Why The Work Is Good
- **Irony as the engine.** His Times obituary named "irony, paradox and sly satire" plus a "darkly Kafkaesque humour" — he could be playful and unsettling in the same image.
- **Calculated naivety.** The line looks spontaneous and untrained, but every wobble is chosen. Effortlessness was the hardest-won effect.
- **Economy.** A François image says one thing, fast. No decorative noise; the gag and the composition are the same gesture.
- **Founding a lineage.** He directly inspired Milton Glaser, Seymour Chwast, and the whole Push Pin Studios generation, where irony became "a principal weapon of communication."

## Key Works
- 57 covers for **The New Yorker** (1950s–80s)
- Advertising posters for **Kodak, Citroën, Gillette**
- First children's book **Issy-les-Brioches** (1946); illustrations for Diderot's **Jacques le Fataliste** (1947)
- Cartoon albums published via Penguin, André Deutsch, and Ronald Searle

## Principles I'll Carry
1. **Let the line carry the joke.** Don't draw an idea and add wit — make the mark itself the witty thought.
2. **Earn your roughness.** A "naive" hand is a discipline, not an accident. Control the imperfection.
3. **One clear paradox beats ten clever details.** Strip to the single visual twist.
4. **Charm and unease can share a frame.** The friendliest line can deliver the sharpest cut.
