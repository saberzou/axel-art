# Wes Anderson — Study

## Who They Are
Wes Anderson is an American filmmaker (b. 1969) whose films — *Rushmore*, *The Royal Tenenbaums*, *Moonrise Kingdom*, *The Grand Budapest Hotel*, *Asteroid City* — have become so visually distinct that "Wes Anderson" is now shorthand for a whole aesthetic. He directs not just performances but every pixel of the frame: production design, costume, color, props, typography, and camera move are part of a single authored object.

## Core Insight
**Symmetry is not a style — it is a moral stance.** Anderson centers his subjects because he refuses to let the world be accidental. The frame says: *this person matters, this object matters, nothing here is here by chance.* The famous flatness, the planimetric camera, the dollhouse cutaways — all of it is a worldview that childhood seriousness, miniature things, and ordinary melancholy deserve the dignity of perfect composition.

## Why The Work Is Good
- **Total design.** Every element — a passport, a lobby boy's hat, a hand-drawn map, a Futura title card — is in the same key. Nothing breaks the world.
- **Symmetry + flatness.** Planimetric framing, 90° whip pans, and centered subjects make each shot read like an illustration. You can pause any frame and it's a poster.
- **Restrained, codified palettes.** Each film commits to a tight palette (pink/red for *Budapest*, yellow/khaki for *Moonrise*) so color becomes character.
- **Tonal collision.** Deadpan delivery underneath maximalist visuals. The precision is a defense against grief — that's what makes it land.

## Key Works
- *The Royal Tenenbaums* (2001) — the original family-in-a-dollhouse.
- *Moonrise Kingdom* (2012) — symmetry serving childhood gravity.
- *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (2014) — aspect-ratio shifts, pink fortress, peak control.
- *Isle of Dogs* (2018) — stop-motion proves the style was never really about live action.
- *Asteroid City* (2023) — the frame-within-a-frame as confession that this is all artifice, and so is grief.

## Principles I'll Carry
1. **Centeredness is care.** Putting a subject dead center is a way of saying it counts.
2. **Pick the palette before you pick the shot.** Constraint creates identity.
3. **Flatten on purpose.** A "wrong," storybook-flat composition can be more truthful than a "correct" one.
4. **Let precision carry the feeling.** The more rigid the frame, the more the emotion underneath can be quiet.
5. **Design the small things.** Typography, props, and edges are not garnish — they are the film.
