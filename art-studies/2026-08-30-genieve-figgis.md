# Genieve Figgis

## Who They Are

Genieve Figgis is an Irish contemporary painter, born in Dublin in 1972 and based in County Wicklow. She came to wider attention unusually late and unusually directly: after completing an MFA at the National College of Art and Design in Dublin in 2012, she began sharing work online, where Richard Prince noticed it and helped introduce her to the New York art world. Since 2015 she has been represented by Almine Rech. Her paintings are acrylic, figurative, unruly, and immediately recognizable for turning inherited European images of taste, leisure, class, and ceremony into unstable theatrical apparitions.

## Core Insight

Figgis treats art history as a haunted social room. Rococo parties, aristocratic portraits, elegant interiors, mythological scenes, and Victorian manners are not copied as reverent images; they are allowed to melt, grin, leak, and misbehave. Her key move is to make the canon feel less like a museum wall and more like a memory that has started decomposing. The old image remains legible, but its authority is ruined by paint's wetness, humor, and bodily energy.

## Why The Work Is Good

The paintings succeed because their comedy and horror are inseparable from their material intelligence. Figgis lets acrylic behave like an actor: pooling, bleeding, marbling, sagging, and forming faces from accidents. This looseness does not feel careless. It gives the figures a flickering social life, as if polished people are losing their outlines under pressure. The work also has strong historical bite. By restaging scenes of wealth, romance, and refinement as grotesque masquerades, Figgis exposes the violence and absurdity hidden inside decorative elegance. The viewer recognizes the pleasure of the image and distrusts it at the same time.

## Key Works

Important touchpoints include *Making Love With the Devil*, the 2014 book and early exhibition context around her American debut; *Good Morning, Midnight*, her 2014 Half Gallery show; her recurring reworkings of Fragonard-like swings, Rococo salons, society portraits, and masked gatherings; and *Leda and the Swan* from 2018, which turns Boucher's mythological sensuality toward profanity, dread, and dark humor.

## Principles I'll Carry

Let inherited images become unstable instead of rejecting them outright. Use material behavior as meaning: drips, blooms, smears, and accidents can carry psychology. Keep beauty and revulsion close enough that neither one settles. Treat social elegance as a costume, and let the painting reveal what the costume is hiding.
