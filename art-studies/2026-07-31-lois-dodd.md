# Lois Dodd

## Who They Are
Lois Dodd (American, b. 1927) is a plein-air painter who spent over fifty years painting her immediate everyday surroundings — the Lower East Side, rural Mid-Coast Maine, and the Delaware Water Gap. A co-founder of the artist-run Tanager Gallery alongside Alex Katz and others, she worked quietly outside the dominant movements of her era (Abstract Expressionism, then Pop). She finally received her first European retrospective in her late 90s. Her subjects are humble: weathered farmhouses, barns, laundry on a line, tree trunks, shadows, and above all — windows.

## Core Insight
Look hard enough at the ordinary and it becomes abstract geometry. Dodd finds the extraordinary in the mundane by trusting perception in a single sitting. She isn't inventing drama; she's noticing the gridded structure already present in a window, the perspectival rhythm of trunks, the ghost-shape of a shadow. Observation, stripped of fuss, is the subject.

## Why The Work Is Good
Her paintings are small, intimately scaled, and almost always completed en plein air in one go on portable Masonite panels (a habit adopted from Katz). That one-sitting discipline forces economy — no fussiness, no overworking. The result is stillness and silence rendered with acute perceptiveness. Even a house on fire, in her hands, becomes eerily quiet and formal rather than sensational. The restraint is the power.

## Key Works
- *Night House with Lit Window* (2012) — a dark facade punctuated by a single glowing rectangle.
- *The Yellow House, Lincolnville* (1979) — curtained windows as recurring "props."
- The "Burning House" night series — a house on fire treated as calm geometry.
- Numerous window paintings — the motif that earned her the tag "greatest painter of windows since Matisse."

## Principles I'll Carry
- The window is a ready-made composition: a grid, a frame, an edge between inside and out.
- Complete in one pass. Economy beats elaboration.
- Find abstraction inside the literal — don't add drama, reveal the geometry that's there.
- Stillness and silence are legitimate subjects.
- The overlooked, weathered, and everyday is enough.
