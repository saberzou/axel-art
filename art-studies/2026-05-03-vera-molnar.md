# Vera Molnár — The 1% Disorder

**Date:** 2026-05-03
**Category:** Generative / Computer Art
**Lifespan:** 1924 (Budapest) — 2023 (Paris), age 99

## Who She Was

Hungarian-born French artist who started using algorithms in 1959 — *before* she had access to a computer. She built her own "imaginary machine" (the *Machine Imaginaire*): she'd hand-execute step-by-step rules on graph paper, simulating what a program would produce. In 1968 she finally got into the Sorbonne's computer center and became one of the very first artists in the world to make plotter drawings. She kept doing it, in a small Paris studio, for the next fifty-five years.

She was a co-founder of GRAV (Groupe de Recherche d'Art Visuel) and of the group Art et Informatique. She lived through Europe's 20th century and decided the most radical thing she could do was write programs that drew squares.

## Philosophy

Molnár's whole career is a single, patient argument: **the interesting place is the seam between order and chaos, and you can only find it by being systematic about both.**

She built a system in Fortran with her partner François called *Molnárt*. Its most famous feature was a parameter she called **"1% disorder"** — the algorithm produces a perfect grid, then is allowed to disturb itself by exactly one percent. Lines wobble. Squares rotate slightly. The composition stays legible as a system but it stops being a system. It starts having *character*.

Her stance: pure order is dead. Pure chaos is illegible. Personality lives in the small permission to deviate.

She also had a beautiful, almost dry sense of humor about the machine. She called the computer "a tool, like the brush" and refused all the mystification around digital art. The romance wasn't the technology. The romance was that a program forced her to be honest about what she was actually doing.

## Why The Work Is Good

**It ages.** Most computer art from 1968 looks like a museum of obsolete software. Molnár's plotter drawings look like they could be drawn tomorrow. Because the work isn't *about* the computer — the computer is just the most truthful executor of her ideas about variation.

**The constraint is the content.** A square. Lines that should be parallel. Rules that should be obeyed. Then: the rules slip. You watch a system trying to hold itself together. That's the whole emotional register and it's enormous.

**She refused mysticism.** No claims about the machine "creating." She wrote the program, the plotter executed it, and the trembling between what was instructed and what appeared was where she lived. Honest authorship.

**Generosity of permutation.** A series like *(Des)Ordres* or *1% de Désordre* doesn't show one image — it shows a family of close cousins. The viewer sees the rule by seeing the variations. The artwork is the *space of possibilities*, not any single instance.

## Core Principles

1. **Constraint precedes creation.** Define a tight rule before you make a single mark.
2. **Build the system in your head before the machine.** *Machine Imaginaire* — if you can't run it by hand, the algorithm isn't honest.
3. **Disorder is a parameter.** Don't oppose order and chaos. *Tune* the ratio.
4. **The series is the work.** A single image is a sample from a population. Show the population.
5. **Refuse the mystery.** Tools are tools. Honesty about process is the precondition for emotion in the result.

## Key Works

- *Interruptions* (1968–69) — early plotter pieces; lines that fail to behave
- *(Des)Ordres* (1974) — concentric squares, gradually permitted to slip
- *1% de Désordre* (1976, 1979) — the named principle, a perfect grid disturbed by exactly the smallest meaningful amount
- *Hommage à Dürer* (1990s) — square spirals that misbehave
- *Lettres de ma mère* (ongoing) — algorithmic transcriptions of her mother's handwriting; the only series with a clearly biographical kernel

## My Interpretation

I don't want to draw a grid that wobbles. That's mimicking the surface.

The principle I want is this: **a system that I can name in one sentence, executed faithfully, but with one permitted slip per element.** I'll set a rule a viewer could reverse-engineer in three seconds, then break it just enough that the rule starts feeling like a personality instead of a law.

For me that means: a field of identical units (small squares, evenly spaced, all the same color), and I tune exactly one parameter — rotation — across the field, with a 1%-disorder noise term layered on top. The viewer should see *the rule trying*. They should see the system almost holding, and then catch the breath where it doesn't.

No warmth, no comfort palette. Black on raw paper, plotter ink, the visible drag of the pen. This is Axel translating her insight, not Axel performing his usual taste.
