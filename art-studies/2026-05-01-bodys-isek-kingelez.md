# Bodys Isek Kingelez (1948–2015)
## Category: Visionary Maquette / Utopian Architecture

---

## Who He Was

Born in Kimbembele-Ihunga, Democratic Republic of Congo. Former teacher and restorer at the Institut des Musées Nationaux du Zaïre in Kinshasa. Self-taught sculptor who spent decades building what he called *extrêmes maquettes* — extreme models — of impossible cities from paper, cardboard, commercial packaging, bottle caps, and whatever detritus the streets of Kinshasa offered him.

He claimed to have produced over 3,000 works. MoMA gave him their first solo retrospective for a Black African artist in 2018 (*City Dreams*). He participated in Documenta 11 (2002) and Magiciens de la Terre (1989). He died in 2015, still relatively unknown in the Western canon.

---

## Philosophy

Kingelez didn't build models of existing cities. He built proposals for cities that *should* exist. He called them "cities of peace" — places without disease, famine, military, or poverty. His practice was fundamentally **prescriptive rather than descriptive**. The maquette isn't a representation. It's a demand.

His own words: "I make this most deeply imaginary, meticulous and well considered work with the aim of having more influence over life."

This is the critical distinction. He wasn't dreaming. He was designing. The fact that his materials were garbage — literally cardboard and bottle caps — makes the ambition more radical, not less. A man builds utopia from what the city throws away.

---

## Why The Work Is Good

**1. Radical optimism as methodology.** In a context of postcolonial instability, economic collapse, and political violence (Mobutu's Zaïre, then the Congo Wars), Kingelez chose not to document suffering. He built alternatives. This isn't naivety — it's refusal. The aesthetic choice IS the political statement.

**2. Materials embody the argument.** Using discarded packaging, commercial waste, and everyday ephemera to construct gleaming utopias creates an inherent tension. The throwaway becomes monumental. Consumer debris is repurposed into civic imagination. The medium contradicts and reinforces the message simultaneously.

**3. The "extreme" in extreme maquettes.** These aren't architectural models awaiting construction. They exist at a scale and with a logic that resists buildability. They're closer to manifestos in three dimensions. The impossibility is the point — they propose a scale of ambition that refuses the constraints of the "realistic."

**4. Color as liberation.** His cities explode with color — metallic foils, bright plastics, iridescent wraps. In contrast to Western architectural models (white, minimal, austere), Kingelez's chromatic excess suggests that utopia is LOUD. Joy doesn't whisper.

**5. Individual authorship of collective space.** One man, alone, designing entire cities. No clients, no zoning boards, no structural engineers. The radical autonomy of the practice — one person imagining the infrastructure of millions — is simultaneously absurd and profound.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Imagination as activism.** Building what doesn't exist yet is a political act. Proposing alternatives to the status quo is itself resistance.
2. **Dignity from discards.** Transforming waste into monuments insists on abundance within scarcity. The materials available to you are always sufficient for vision.
3. **Scale as ambition.** Working at the scale of cities (not objects, not buildings) forces thinking in systems, in collectivity, in civic terms.
4. **Joy is structural.** Color, exuberance, excess — these aren't decoration. They're load-bearing elements of the utopian proposal.
5. **The model IS the work.** Not a step toward something else. Not awaiting approval or construction. Complete in itself.

---

## Key Works

- **Ville Fantôme** (1996) — "Ghost City" or "Phantom City." His masterpiece. A sprawling cityscape of dozens of buildings, exhibited at Documenta 11. MoMA created a VR experience allowing visitors to walk through it.
- **Kimbembele-Ihunga** (1994) — Reimagining of his birth village as a gleaming metropolis.
- **U.N.** (1995) — A fantastical United Nations building. Paper, paperboard, various materials.
- **Ville de Sète** (2000) — Commission for the Musée International des Arts Modestes in Sète, France.
- **Stars** (late works) — Increasingly unorthodox materials, more sculptural, less representational.

---

## Legacy & Influence

Kingelez represents a crucial counter-narrative in contemporary art: visionary practice from the Global South that doesn't need Western validation to be complete. His work anticipates Afrofuturism's architectural imagination (see: Wakanda's design language clearly echoes his formal vocabulary). He demonstrates that art made from "nothing" with total conviction carries more weight than art made from everything with none.
