# Suzy Lee

## Who They Are

Suzy Lee is a Seoul-born Korean picture-book artist, illustrator, and author, trained in painting at Seoul National University and in book arts at Camberwell College of Arts in London. She is best known for wordless picture books, especially the "Border Trilogy": *Mirror* (2003), *Wave* (2008), and *Shadow* (2010). In 2022 she received the Hans Christian Andersen Award for Illustration, a career-level recognition for children's literature. Her work sits between illustration, artists' books, performance, and object design: the page is not just a surface for images, but a hinge, a stage, and a boundary.

## Core Insight

Lee's central invention is treating the physical book as part of the story. The gutter, page turn, blank margin, and spread are active forces. A child meets a reflection, a wave, or a shadow, and the encounter becomes a game with the boundary between real and imagined space. The books often need few or no words because the drama is already in sequence, scale, rhythm, and touch.

## Why The Work Is Good

The work is spare without feeling empty. Lee builds enormous emotional range from simple graphic contrasts: charcoal black against white, blue water against negative space, a small body facing an immense field. Her restraint gives readers room to participate. Instead of explaining wonder, she designs situations where wonder can happen through looking. The images understand childhood play as serious investigation: a child tests a line, crosses it, retreats, repeats, and discovers a world by moving through it.

## Key Works

* *Mirror* (2003): a wordless encounter with reflection, symmetry, and estrangement.
* *Wave* (2008): a girl and the sea staged across the book's centerfold.
* *Shadow* (2010): ordinary objects become a theatrical underworld of silhouettes.
* *Lines* (2017): skating marks become drawing, movement, error, and renewal.
* *Summer* (2021/2022 editions): music, water, and play translated into picture-book rhythm.

## Principles I'll Carry

Use the frame as a participant, not a container. Let negative space carry suspense. Make one boundary do narrative work. Prefer a clear physical action over symbolic explanation. Build emotion through rhythm: approach, contact, rupture, return. Give the viewer enough silence to finish the image internally.
