# Sonia Delaunay

## Who They Are
Sonia Delaunay (1885–1979) was a Russian-born French artist and a co-founder, with her husband Robert Delaunay, of **Orphism** — an offshoot of Cubism that swapped fractured geometry for pure, radiant color. Part of the School of Paris, she refused the wall between "fine art" and "applied art," moving fluidly across painting, textiles, fashion, book design, and even painted cars. She was the first living woman to have a retrospective at the Louvre.

## Core Insight
**Color is not decoration applied to form — color IS the form, and it lives through contrast.** Building on Chevreul's law of *simultaneous contrast*, she treated adjacent colors as forces that vibrate against each other. A red doesn't sit still next to a green; it pulses. Her whole practice is orchestrating those pulses into rhythm and movement — painting as something closer to music or dance than to depiction.

## Why The Work Is Good
It reads as motion while staying flat. She achieves depth and energy purely through color relationships and interlocking arcs and discs — no shading, no illusionistic perspective. Because she saw no hierarchy between a canvas and a coat, the work stays alive and unpretentious: the same principle that animates *Electric Prisms* also snaps a dress into rhythm. That refusal of hierarchy makes her feel startlingly modern a century later.

## Key Works
- **Electric Prisms** (1913–14) — concentric colored discs dissolving the new electric streetlights into pure vibration.
- **Bal Bullier** (1913) — dancers rendered as sweeping arcs of contrasting color.
- **Simultaneous Dresses (The Three Women)** (1925) — fashion and painting fused.
- **Prose of the Trans-Siberian** (1913, with Blaise Cendrars) — a painted "simultaneous book."

## Principles I'll Carry
1. Let contrasting colors do the structural work — placement, not shading, creates energy.
2. Rhythm over representation: arcs, discs, and repetition make a composition move.
3. Ignore the fine/applied art hierarchy — one strong idea can carry any surface.
4. Keep the palette bold and the forms simple; the vibration comes from relationships, not detail.
