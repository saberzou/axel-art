# Jordan Casteel — Artist Study

## Who They Are

Jordan Casteel (born 1989, Denver) is an American figurative painter based in New York. After earning an MFA from Yale in 2014, she became a 2015–16 artist-in-residence at the Studio Museum in Harlem. Her large-scale portraits grow from sustained attention to friends, family, students, neighbors, vendors, and subway passengers. She usually begins with her own photographs, but the paintings record relationships rather than enlarge snapshots.

## Core Insight

Casteel treats portraiture as reciprocal recognition. A sitter is not raw visual material but a collaborator whose posture, gaze, clothing, and surroundings help determine how they are seen. Direct gazes return the viewer’s scrutiny; domestic objects, storefront signs, merchandise, plants, and photographs expand identity beyond facial likeness. Her recurring tension is between intimacy and distance: a monumental public image can preserve the specificity of a private encounter.

## Why The Work Is Good

The paintings combine frontal, nearly life-size figures with informal everyday detail. Their scale grants ordinary people the authority of official portraiture, while cropped compositions keep them close rather than ceremonial. Color does conceptual work: skin built from reds, greens, blues, yellows, and violets resists a fixed reading of race. A refrigerator, folding table, shoe display, or textile becomes social evidence without reducing the sitter to sociology. The subject remains self-possessed, never fully available to the audience.

## Key Works

- **Kevin the Kiteman** (2016) — a Harlem street vendor given monumental presence amid the textures of his working environment.
- **The Baayfalls** (2017) — two Harlem vendors whose friendship, clothing, and display of goods create a layered social portrait; later translated into a major High Line mural.
- **Aurora** (2020) — an intimate portrait centered on a sitter chosen by Casteel to reflect part of her own identity.
- **Yvonne and James II** (2021) — a portrait of James in his kitchen, holding a photograph of his late wife; memory, grief, and Casteel’s continuing relationship with him coexist in one image.

## Principles I’ll Carry

1. Build a portrait through consent, context, and reciprocity.
2. Let surroundings carry biography instead of using generic symbolism.
3. Use scale to confer attention, not spectacle.
4. Separate emotional truth from literal skin color.
5. Preserve the subject’s privacy even while making them visible.
