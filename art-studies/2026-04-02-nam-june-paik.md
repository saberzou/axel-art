# Nam June Paik — Video Art / New Media
*Studied: 2026-04-02*

## Who He Was

Nam June Paik (1932–2006). Born in Seoul, Korea. Fled the Korean War, studied music and art history in Japan and Germany. Ended up in New York. Became the undisputed father of video art.

Trained as a classical musician, fell in with the Fluxus movement in the early 60s. His first solo show in 1963 at Galerie Parnass featured 13 modified TV sets displaying distorted images — "Exposition of Music – Electronic Television." That show basically invented the medium.

## Why His Work Is Good

Paik understood something nobody else did at the time: **television was not a window, it was a material.** Just as painters use pigment and sculptors use stone, Paik treated the cathode ray tube as raw substance to be shaped, bent, and subverted.

What makes his work age well is that it predicted our entire relationship with screens. In 1974 he coined "electronic superhighway" — 20 years before the internet went mainstream. His "Electronic Superhighway: Continental U.S., Alaska, Hawaii" (1995) is a wall of 336 TVs mapping America through its own media output. It feels more relevant now than when he made it.

His work stays alive because it asks questions we still can't answer: What happens when we watch ourselves watching? Is technology a liberation or a cage? Can ancient wisdom survive the electronic age?

## Core Principles

### 1. Technology Is Only Interesting When You Break It
Paik didn't use TV as intended. He used magnets to warp signals. He stacked sets into robots. He buried monitors in gardens. The tool becomes art only when you refuse to use it "correctly." Every piece is an act of creative disobedience.

### 2. The Observer Is the Observed
TV Buddha (1974) — a bronze Buddha statue watching its own live image on a closed-circuit TV. This single piece encapsulates everything: meditation meets surveillance, ancient contemplation meets modern narcissism, presence meets representation. The loop never resolves. It's the selfie camera 40 years early.

### 3. East/West Is a False Binary
Paik called one of his satellite broadcasts "Bye Bye Kipling" — a direct rejection of "East is East, and West is West, and never the twain shall meet." His entire practice was proof that Zen Buddhism and electronic media, Korean shamanism and Fluxus performance, could inhabit the same space without contradiction.

### 4. Participation Over Consumption
He built the Paik-Abe Video Synthesizer (1969) and invited gallery visitors to use it, to perform in front of cameras, to manipulate their own footage. He called television a "dictatorial medium" and spent his career making it democratic. The audience creates the work.

### 5. Nature and Technology Are Not Opposites
TV Garden (1974) — monitors scattered among living tropical plants, screens glowing with abstract video among leaves and soil. No conflict between organic and electronic. They're just different expressions of the same energy.

## Key Works

- **Exposition of Music – Electronic Television** (1963) — the show that started it all
- **Magnet TV** (1965) — a magnet placed on a TV, warping broadcast signals into abstract patterns
- **TV Buddha** (1974) — the Buddha watching itself on closed-circuit TV
- **TV Garden** (1974) — monitors embedded in living plants
- **Electronic Superhighway** (1995) — 336 TVs mapping the US, neon outlining state borders
- **The More, The Better** (1988) — 1,003 TV monitors stacked into a tower at MMCA Korea
- **Paik-Abe Video Synthesizer** (1969) — the instrument that let anyone become a video artist

## The Deeper Insight

Paik's real genius wasn't about screens or signals. It was about **feedback loops**. Every piece creates a loop: viewer watches screen watches viewer. Buddha watches Buddha. Nature grows through technology. East becomes West becomes East. His work says: all experience is circular, all observation changes what's observed, and the only honest response to a medium that claims to show you "reality" is to shatter the illusion by showing the medium itself.

We live in Paik's world now. Every Zoom call where we stare at our own face, every Instagram scroll, every algorithmic mirror. He saw it all coming and his response wasn't fear. It was play.
