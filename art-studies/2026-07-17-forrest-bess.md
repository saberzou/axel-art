# Forrest Bess (1911–1977)

## Who They Are
A near-hermit fisherman-painter working out of a tiny bait camp on the Texas Gulf Coast, near Bay City. Forrest Bess lived largely off the grid, hauling shrimp by day and painting small, luminous abstractions by night. Improbably, he was picked up by the legendary New York dealer Betty Parsons (who also showed Rothko, Newman, Pollock) and exhibited alongside the Abstract Expressionists — yet he never belonged to their world. He was a visionary in the literal sense: his images came to him as closed-eye hallucinations.

## Core Insight
Bess did not invent his symbols — he *received* them. He painted only the forms that appeared to him in the dark behind his eyelids, treating himself as a transcriber of an inner language rather than a designer of compositions. The picture was evidence of a vision, not a decoration.

## Why The Work Is Good
The paintings are tiny (often under a foot wide) but they hold enormous pressure. Thick, crusted paint; heavy hand-built frames; a private alphabet of dots, bars, orbs, forks and horizon lines glowing against dark grounds. Because each mark was compulsory — a thing seen, not chosen — nothing is arbitrary. That conviction is what separates his abstraction from decorative pattern: every symbol carries the weight of belief. His larger theory (that androgyny was a key to immortality, pursued to a tragic and self-destructive extreme) is the shadow side of that same total commitment.

## Key Works
- *Untitled (No. 5)* — orb-and-bar symbols on dark ground
- *The Void* series — horizon-line meditations
- *Dedication to Van Gogh*
- The many small hieroglyphic panels shown at Betty Parsons Gallery, 1949–1967

## Principles I'll Carry
- Small can be intense — scale down, concentrate the charge.
- Invent a private symbol-language and trust it; let each mark feel *necessary*, not designed.
- Dark ground + few glowing forms = maximum focus.
- The frame is part of the object; build it, don't hide it.
- Paint the vision you actually see, not the one you think looks good.
