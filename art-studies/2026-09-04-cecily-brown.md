# Cecily Brown

## Who They Are
Cecily Brown is a British painter born in London in 1969 and based in New York. Trained at the Slade School of Fine Art, she became known in the 1990s for paintings that refused to choose between figuration and abstraction. Her work is often discussed in relation to Rubens, Goya, Fragonard, Poussin, de Kooning, Bacon, and Joan Mitchell, but it feels contemporary because it treats those references as unstable matter rather than fixed quotation. Recent institutional attention includes "Death and the Maid" at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, "Themes and Variations" at the Barnes Foundation, and "Picture Making" at Serpentine in London.

## Core Insight
Brown's core insight is that a painting can withhold its subject without becoming empty. She builds scenes where bodies, animals, interiors, gardens, and historical motifs seem to surface, dissolve, and re-form inside the same field. The viewer is not given a clean image to consume; looking becomes an active, physical search. Desire, violence, comedy, and decay arrive as glimpses in motion.

## Why The Work Is Good
The work is good because its energy is not just expressionist noise. Under the apparent turbulence are strong compositional pressures: diagonals, clustered figures, directional strokes, and color temperatures that push the eye around the canvas. Brown understands paint as both image and event. A mark can be flesh, foliage, shadow, or pure speed depending on how long it is watched. This makes the paintings unusually durable: they reward first impact, then slow re-reading.

## Key Works
Key works and bodies of work include "Four Letter Heaven" (1995), the early bunny paintings shown at Deitch Projects, "Trouble in Paradise" (1999), "Puttin' on the Ritz" (1999-2000), the "Black Paintings" of the early 2000s, "Fair of Face, Full of Woe" (2008), "The Triumph of Death" (2019), and later pastoral or garden-inflected works connected to Blenheim Palace and Serpentine.

## Principles I'll Carry
Let recognition arrive late. Make brushwork carry narrative pressure, not decoration. Use historical motifs as compost rather than citation. Keep the image in a productive state of becoming: enough structure to invite reading, enough ambiguity to keep the eye moving. Treat beauty and unease as simultaneous, not opposing, forces.
