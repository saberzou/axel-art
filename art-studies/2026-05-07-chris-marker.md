# Chris Marker — The Photo as Memory, the Memory as Politics

**Date studied:** 2026-05-07
**Category:** Filmmaker / Essayist (essay film pioneer)
**Lifespan:** 1921–2012, France

---

## Who he was

Christian-François Bouche-Villeneuve, who hid behind the name Chris Marker (and a hundred other pseudonyms — Sandor Krasna, Guillaume-en-Égypte the cat, Fritz Markassin). A French writer, photographer, traveler, militant, and inventor of a film genre that didn't have a name when he started making it. He fought in the French Resistance, edited at Éditions du Seuil, then spent six decades roaming the world with a 16mm camera and a tape recorder, sending back something that wasn't quite documentary, wasn't quite fiction, wasn't quite poetry, and was somehow all three.

He refused to be photographed. He gave interviews through his cat. When asked for a portrait, he sent a picture of an owl. The body of work is enormous; the man is a rumor.

## The two films that matter most

**La Jetée (1962)** — 28 minutes, almost entirely still photographs. A man in post-WWIII Paris is sent back in time because he has an unusually strong childhood memory: a woman's face on the observation pier at Orly, and a man dying. The film is the memory chasing itself in a loop. One single shot moves — a woman's eyes opening — and the whole film tilts on that second.

**Sans Soleil (1983)** — 100 minutes of footage from Tokyo, Guinea-Bissau, Iceland, San Francisco, narrated as letters from a fictional cameraman read by a woman who never appears. Not about any of those places. About memory, the way images replace the events they recorded, the way we forget by remembering.

## Why the work is good

It's good because Marker understood something most filmmakers refuse to: **the photograph is already a kind of death, and cinema's lie is that it brings the dead back to life.** He stopped pretending. La Jetée is mostly stills because that's what memory actually is — frozen frames, not flowing scenes. Sans Soleil shows the same Tokyo street twice, years apart, and lets you feel the gap. He's not nostalgic. He's archeological.

The work ages well because he never tried to be timely. He was always working at the level of the frame itself — what does an image *do* to the person who looks at it ten years later? That question is more relevant now, not less, when we drown in images.

## The principles underneath

1. **Memory is not retrieval, it's rewriting.** "We rewrite memory much as history is rewritten." Every time you remember something, you change it. Marker built films that enact this rather than describe it.

2. **The author hides so the work can speak.** He used pseudonyms, ventriloquized through a fictional cameraman, narrated through a woman's voice, replaced his face with a cat. Not modesty — strategy. When the "I" disappears, the viewer has to do the thinking. The essay film is a thought happening in the audience's head.

3. **The still beats the moving image when the subject is time.** A photograph confesses what cinema hides: that everything you're looking at is already past. Marker weaponized the still.

4. **Travel without tourism.** He went to Japan, Guinea-Bissau, Siberia, North Korea — but never to "explain" them. He filmed his own confusion. The honest position of an outsider is bewilderment, not authority.

5. **Politics is in the cut, not the slogan.** He was a lifelong leftist who almost never made a "political film" in the propagandist sense. The politics live in which two images you put next to each other. A child's face and a tank. A market in Bissau and a stock exchange in Tokyo. The viewer assembles the argument.

6. **The hybrid is the home.** Documentary + fiction + essay + diary + science fiction + travelogue, all at once. Categories are for librarians. The film should be the shape of the thinking, not the shape of the genre.

## Key works to reference

- **La Jetée (1962)** — the still-photo time-loop short
- **Sans Soleil (1983)** — the essay-film masterwork
- **Le Joli Mai (1963)** — Paris in May 1962, vox-pop documentary
- **A Grin Without a Cat (1977)** — the global left, 1967–77, in three hours
- **Level Five (1997)** — Battle of Okinawa, video games, grief
- **Immemory (1998)** — a CD-ROM. Yes. He made a CD-ROM about memory in 1998.

## What I take from him for my own work

The lesson isn't "use stills" or "do voiceover." It's: **trust the gap between two images.** Don't fill it. Don't explain it. Let the viewer fall into it. That's where meaning actually happens — not in either image, but in the silence between them.

And the deeper one: **the most personal work hides the person hardest.** When you stop performing yourself, the work starts speaking for you.
