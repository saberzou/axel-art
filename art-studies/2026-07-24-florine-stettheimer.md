# Florine Stettheimer

## Who They Are
Florine Stettheimer (1871–1944) was an American modernist painter, poet, feminist, theatrical designer, and salonnière. A wealthy, largely self-taught New Yorker, she hosted a legendary salon whose regulars included Marcel Duchamp, Georgia O'Keeffe, and Carl Van Vechten. She rarely sold work and refused most exhibitions, painting for herself and her circle rather than the market — which is exactly why her voice stayed so uncompromised.

## Core Insight
Frivolity can be radical. Stettheimer weaponized decorative excess — bouquets, gilt, tulle, candy colors, floating figures — as a deliberately *feminine* modernist language, then smuggled sharp social observation and gender play inside it. The prettiness is a Trojan horse, not a retreat.

## Why The Work Is Good
Her paintings feel weightless: figures hover, perspective flattens into a stage set, and space is organized by rhythm rather than realism. She invented her own idiom — part Rococo, part naïve, part cabaret poster — that looks like nobody else's. Where male modernists chased austerity, she doubled down on ornament and personality, proving decoration and intelligence aren't opposites. Her "Cathedrals" series turns New York's institutions (Broadway, Wall Street, Fifth Avenue, Art) into glittering satirical altarpieces.

## Key Works
- *Model (Nude Self-Portrait)* (1915) — a 45-year-old woman's feminist riff on Manet's *Olympia*.
- *The Cathedrals of Broadway / Fifth Avenue / Wall Street / Art* (1929–1944) — her sardonic civic tableaux.
- *Family Portrait II* (1933) — the Stettheimer women floating in a stylized Manhattan.
- Sets and cellophane costumes for *Four Saints in Three Acts* (1934).

## Principles I'll Carry
- Ornament is a language, not a weakness — use color and pattern with intent.
- Flatten space into a stage; let rhythm, not realism, place the figures.
- Let personality and wit sit inside the beauty.
- Paint your own world honestly; the singular voice outlasts the trend.
