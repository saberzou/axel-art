# Beatriz Milhazes — Painting / Pattern

## Who They Are
Beatriz Milhazes (b. 1960, Rio de Janeiro) is a Brazilian painter and collage artist famous for large-scale, kaleidoscopic canvases that detonate with color. She juxtaposes Brazilian visual culture — carnival, colonial Baroque churches, tropical flora, lace, crochet, candy wrappers — against the geometry and abstraction of Western Modernism (Bridget Riley's op-art, Matisse's cut-outs, Tarsila do Amaral's Brazilian modernism). Represented by White Cube; collected by the Guggenheim, MoMA, and Tate.

## Core Insight
The hand of the painter should disappear so the *pattern* can sing. Milhazes invented a signature transfer technique: she paints each motif in acrylic onto a sheet of clear plastic, lets it dry, then glues that painted film face-down onto the canvas and peels the plastic away — leaving the paint behind with a flat, brushstroke-free surface. The peel often cracks or leaves faint craquelure, and she welcomes these "irregularities" as a memory imprinted into the work. Beloved motifs are saved, repainted, and reused across paintings like a personal alphabet. The result is mechanical smoothness fused with organic accident.

## Why The Work Is Good
It resolves a tension most decorative art can't: it is unapologetically pretty AND structurally rigorous. The winding, lasso-like composition pulls your eye on a spiral journey while competing fields of pattern (targets dissolving into lace dissolving into flowers) hold a taut balance. Nothing reads as flat wallpaper because every layer carries the trace of its making. Joy is treated as a serious formal subject.

## Key Works
- **Pó de arroz** (2017–18) — circles coil into bull's-eye targets, then revert to crochet detail.
- **O Mágico** (2001) — Guggenheim; arabesques + geometry + floral collage.
- **Marola, Succulent, Gamboa** series — large carnival-bright tondos and panels.
- **Collages** layering chocolate wrappers and branded packaging into unified motion.

## Principles I'll Carry
1. Hide the brush so the system speaks — flatness can feel alive if the process leaves traces.
2. Build a reusable vocabulary of motifs; remix beloved shapes across works.
3. Let competing pattern-fields fight, then unify them with one winding line of movement.
4. Welcome the crack, the peel, the accident — irregularity = memory.
5. Treat joy and color as rigorous formal problems, not decoration.
