# Julie Morstad

## Who They Are

Julie Morstad is a Canadian author, artist, and illustrator based in Vancouver, best known for picture books that make childhood perception feel precise, strange, and quietly expansive. She came through the Vancouver arts scene and began illustrating books in the mid-2000s, later writing and illustrating her own titles. Her work moves across children's books, editorial illustration, animation, design, and cover art, but the picture book remains her strongest arena because it lets her pace visual discovery across time.

## Core Insight

Morstad's core insight is that small observations can become whole worlds when the image gives them enough room. She often builds scenes from modest gestures: a body leaning, a garment floating, a room flattened like a stage, a child watching something adults might miss. The result is not spectacle. It is attention turned into atmosphere.

## Why The Work Is Good

Her illustrations are delicate without being weak. Fine graphite lines, pale color, gouache, ink, pencil, and digital layering create surfaces that feel hand-touched and breathable. She has a strong sense of costume, pattern, and historical setting, but the research never stiffens the drawing. In books such as *Swan*, she uses stage-like flatness and gesture to connect composition to subject: Anna Pavlova's life is not just depicted; it is choreographed on the page. White space is equally important. Morstad knows when to leave a figure alone, when to let a vignette hover, and when a small burst of color should carry the emotional beat.

## Key Works

Key works include *How To*, *Today*, *Time Is a Flower*, *Swan: The Life and Dance of Anna Pavlova*, *Bloom: A Story of Fashion Designer Elsa Schiaparelli*, *It Began with a Page: How Gyo Fujikawa Drew the Way*, *House of Dreams: The Life of L.M. Montgomery*, and *This Is Sadie*. These books show her range: instructional play, biography, seasonal time, fashion, interior imagination, and quiet narrative portraiture.

## Principles I'll Carry

Let the image breathe. Treat clothing, rooms, and objects as emotional actors. Use limited depth when the scene wants theatrical clarity. Keep gesture alive through revision. Make research visible through specificity, not clutter. Trust a small observation if it has been looked at deeply enough.
