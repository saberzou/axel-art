# Kitty Crowther

## Who They Are

Kitty Crowther is a Belgian author and illustrator of picture books, born in Brussels in 1970 to an English father and Swedish mother. She studied graphic arts at Institut Saint-Luc in Brussels and has been making children's books since 1994. Her body of work, now more than forty books, earned the 2010 Astrid Lindgren Memorial Award, where she was praised as a master of both line and atmosphere.

## Core Insight

Crowther's books treat childhood not as a simplified emotional zone, but as a place where loneliness, fear, grief, tenderness, and oddness can all sit together. Her great move is to make the uncanny feel intimate. Animals, insects, small girls, shadows, darkness, lakes, and strange presences appear without theatrical shock. They are drawn as if the reader has always known them. The fantastic is not an escape from ordinary life; it is the shape ordinary life takes when seen honestly by a child.

## Why The Work Is Good

Her images are built from modest tools: pencil, ink, colored pencil, and open space. That restraint lets every wobble of line carry feeling. Faces are rarely over-described, but posture, scale, and rhythm do the emotional work. She also trusts quiet pacing. A page can hold suspense through a dark room, a small lamp, a repeated sound, or the pause before someone speaks. Her palette often feels nocturnal and mineral, with sudden acidic or warm notes that make the scene breathe. The result is work that is gentle without being soft-minded. It gives children complexity without explaining it away.

## Key Works

Important books include *Mon royaume*, her 1994 debut; *Mon ami Jim*; *Scritch scratch dip clapote!*; *La visite de la Petite Mort*; the *Poka & Mine* books; *Alors?*; *Annie du lac*, winner of the 2009 Prix Baobab; *Le petit homme et Dieu*; and *Petites histoires de nuit* / *Stories of the Night*.

## Principles I'll Carry

Use simple drawing materials with emotional precision. Let darkness be a place of listening, not just danger. Make strange figures feel domestic and close. Leave room around characters so silence becomes part of the composition. Handle fear, grief, and solitude with respect, but keep a small lamp of warmth somewhere in the scene.
