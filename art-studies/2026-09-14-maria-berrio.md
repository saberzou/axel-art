# María Berrío — Artist Study

## Who They Are

María Berrío (born 1982 in Bogotá, Colombia) is a Brooklyn-based painter and collage artist known for monumental images assembled from Japanese paper and watercolor. Her imagined worlds draw on the Colombian landscape, magical-realist literature, oral tradition, myth, and migration. Women and children occupy the center—not as symbols of helplessness, but as witnesses, survivors, and makers of refuge.

## Core Insight

Berrío treats collage as world-building. Each fragment carries its own pattern, fiber, history, and edge; layered together, these discontinuities become a coherent place. That logic mirrors her subject: identity, memory, and belonging are accumulations. Lushness creates imaginative space for displacement, grief, ecological anxiety, and survival without reducing people to their suffering.

## Why The Work Is Good

The work succeeds through contradiction. Its surfaces shimmer with abundance, while its figures remain still, guarded, or psychologically distant. Scale gives them authority. Dense foliage, animals, textiles, and patterned skies feel protective and unstable at once. A scene may suggest migration, aftermath, ritual, or rebirth without closing into illustration. Building piece by piece lets discoveries in the paper redirect the image. Repair and rupture coexist in the visible joins.

## Key Works

- *A Sunburst Restrained* (2019): reclining women inhabit a pastel interior charged by a lemon branch.
- *Self-Portrait* (2019): a rare direct act of self-representation within her larger practice.
- *Closed Geometry* (2022): a displaced woman rests in a refuge where regeneration has begun.
- *Ozymandias* (2022): a large paper-and-watercolor collage whose title invokes ruin and impermanence.
- *El Dorado* (2024), *Songlines* (2025), and *Edge of the Salted Plains* (2025): recent works that loosen figuration into mobile, abstract passages.

## Principles I'll Carry

1. Let material structure embody the idea rather than merely decorate it.
2. Build beauty and unease into the same image.
3. Give vulnerable figures presence, agency, and psychological privacy.
4. Use fragments to make a world whose seams remain visible.
5. Keep narrative open enough for viewers to enter and complete it.

Sources: National Gallery of Art; The Metropolitan Museum of Art; Brooklyn Museum; Minneapolis Institute of Art; San José Museum of Art; Norton Museum of Art.
