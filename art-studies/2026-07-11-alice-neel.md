# Alice Neel — Study (2026-07-11)

## Who They Are
Alice Neel (1900–1984) was an American figurative painter, widely considered one of the greatest portraitists of the 20th century. She spent decades painting people — lovers, neighbors, pregnant women, art-world figures, the sick, the marginalized — from her apartments in Spanish Harlem and the Upper West Side. She worked stubbornly in a realist figurative mode through the whole reign of Abstract Expressionism, ignored by the market for most of her life, and was finally celebrated in old age.

## Core Insight
A portrait is not a record of a surface — it's a collision between two psychologies, the sitter's and the painter's. Neel called herself a "collector of souls." She sought the inner life *above* physical likeness, willing to distort color, exaggerate a gesture, or leave a hand unfinished if that got closer to who the person actually was.

## Why The Work Is Good
Neel resolved a tension most painters treat as either/or: her portraits are simultaneously honest, recognizable *documents* and loose, expressionistic, almost "sloppy" paintings. The blue outline around a body, the acid green in a shadow, the empty raw canvas left showing — these aren't decoration, they're psychological pressure. She refused traditional categories (age, race, gender, status) and painted the powerful and the forgotten with the same unflinching, tender scrutiny. Bodies sag, eyes are wary, vulnerability is never airbrushed. The result feels alive because it feels *seen*.

## Key Works
- *Andy Warhol* (1970) — the wounded icon, scars and corset bared, eyes closed.
- *Pregnant Woman* / her many pregnant nudes — bodies rarely painted honestly.
- *T.B. Harlem* (1940) — dignity in illness.
- *Self-Portrait* (1980) — naked at 80, brush in hand, ruthless about herself.

## Principles I'll Carry
1. Chase the *psychology*, not the likeness — distort freely if it gets you closer to the truth of a person.
2. Let the process show. Raw canvas, a nervous outline, an unfinished hand are honesty, not error.
3. Use color as emotion, not description — an acid shadow can carry a mood a "correct" one can't.
4. Grant every subject the same unflinching, tender attention, regardless of status.
