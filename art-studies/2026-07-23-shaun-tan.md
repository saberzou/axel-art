# Shaun Tan

## Who They Are
Shaun Tan (b. 1974, Perth, Australia) is an illustrator, author, and film-maker best known for wordless and near-wordless picture books that read as literature for all ages. His landmark work *The Arrival* (2007) tells a migration story entirely through sepia-toned images; other key titles include *The Red Tree*, *The Lost Thing*, *Tales from Outer Suburbia*, and *The Rabbits*. He won the Astrid Lindgren Memorial Award (ALMA) and an Academy Award for the animated short of *The Lost Thing*.

## Core Insight
Tan uses the *fantastic* not as escapism but as a truer lens on ordinary emotion. He builds invented worlds — strange animals, impossible architecture, unreadable scripts — so the reader experiences dislocation *from the inside*. A migrant can't read the signs; neither can we. The imaginary becomes the most honest way to depict displacement, belonging, and quiet melancholy.

## Why The Work Is Good
- **Empathy through disorientation.** By making everything alien, he forces the viewer to feel the newcomer's confusion rather than merely observe it.
- **Silence as a tool.** Removing text makes images do the emotional labor; pacing, gesture, and scale carry the whole narrative.
- **Autobiographical undercurrent.** A recurring theme of "being different, not fitting in, finding yourself on strange, precarious territory" gives the surreal genuine weight.
- **Craft range.** He moves fluidly between graphite realism, oil-painted texture, and sculptural whimsy — technique always serves feeling.

## Key Works
- *The Arrival* (2007) — wordless migration epic, sepia photorealism meets surreal fauna.
- *The Red Tree* (2001) — depression rendered as a wandering girl in shifting dreamscapes.
- *The Lost Thing* (2000 / 2010 film) — bureaucratic suburbia hiding a forgotten creature.
- *Tales from Outer Suburbia* (2008) — small magic in mundane backyards.

## Principles I'll Carry
1. Invent a world to make a familiar feeling *newly felt*.
2. Let a single figure be dwarfed by scale — smallness reads as vulnerability.
3. Ground the surreal with photoreal texture so the strange feels lived-in.
4. Trust silence; the strongest emotion often needs no caption.
