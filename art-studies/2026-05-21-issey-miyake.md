# Issey Miyake — 2026-05-21

## Who They Are
Issey Miyake (1938–2022) was a Japanese fashion designer whose practice sat closer to material science and industrial design than to couture. A Hiroshima survivor, he trained in Paris in the 1960s, then returned to Tokyo and built a studio that treated cloth — not the body, not the silhouette — as the primary subject of design. His most famous projects, *Pleats Please* (1993) and *A-POC: A Piece Of Cloth* (1998, with Dai Fujiwara), reframed clothing as a system rather than a garment.

## Core Insight
**Start at the thread, not the body.** Most designers begin with a figure and drape fabric around it. Miyake began with a single piece of cloth — its weave, its memory, its capacity to hold form — and let the garment emerge from what the material itself wanted to do. The body comes last, and the cloth completes itself only when worn.

## Why The Work Is Good
It's good because the *method* is visible in the result. A Pleats Please dress is honest about being heat-set polyester; the pleats are not ornament, they're the structural logic of how the piece holds shape, packs flat, and moves. A-POC tubes come off an industrial knitting machine as a single continuous program — the wearer cuts the garment free. The work collapses the distance between factory, designer, and user, and it does so without any decorative apology. Lightness, repeatability, low waste, and bodily freedom all fall out of one rigorous idea.

## Key Works
- **Pleats Please** (1993–) — pleats applied *after* sewing, garment as compressed memory.
- **A-POC** (1998, with Dai Fujiwara) — a single thread, a single tube, infinite cuts.
- **Bao Bao** (2000) — triangulated mesh bag that flattens and re-forms with contents.
- **132 5.** (2010) — folded flat origami garments that bloom into 3D when worn.
- **Making Things** exhibition (Fondation Cartier, 1998) — practice shown as research.

## Principles I'll Carry
1. **Begin with the substrate.** Decide what the material can do before deciding what the thing should look like.
2. **Process is the ornament.** If the manufacturing logic is honest enough, it doesn't need decoration.
3. **A flat plane that becomes a volume** is more interesting than a volume that pretends to be itself.
4. **Design for the moment of use, not the moment of display.** The piece should only resolve when activated.
5. **Repetition with variation** — a single geometric unit, scaled and rotated, can carry an entire identity.
