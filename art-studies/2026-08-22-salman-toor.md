# Salman Toor

## Who They Are

Salman Toor is a Lahore-born, New York-based painter, born in 1983, whose work centers queer South Asian men in intimate, imagined scenes between private rooms, bars, checkpoints, bedrooms, and art-historical memory. He studied at Ohio Wesleyan University and Pratt Institute, and his first museum solo exhibition, *How Will I Know*, appeared at the Whitney Museum of American Art in 2020-2021. Later projects such as *No Ordinary Love* brought his recent paintings and works on paper into conversation with European collection histories.

## Core Insight

Toor's strongest idea is that art history can be entered through tenderness instead of conquest. He borrows the compositional charge of Old Master painting, genre scenes, and theatrical light, but redirects that inheritance toward brown, queer, diasporic presence. The result is not quotation for its own sake; it is a change in who gets to be pictured as luminous, awkward, bored, desired, scrutinized, or at home.

## Why The Work Is Good

The paintings feel loose and fast while being deeply informed. A figure may be built from economical strokes, but the staging knows exactly where attention should rest: a hand near a phone, a green-lit face, a slouched body in a social room, a security encounter that makes the air tighten. Toor's greens often act like emotional weather, giving ordinary scenes a nocturnal, unstable glow. He is especially good at making ambiguity feel humane. His people are not symbols first; they are caught in partial stories, carrying vulnerability, vanity, wit, and self-protection at once.

## Key Works

Important works and projects include *How Will I Know* at the Whitney; *No Ordinary Love*, first organized with the Baltimore Museum of Art and later shown elsewhere; *Museum Boys*, placed at the Frick Collection in dialogue with Vermeer; *The Bar on East 13th Street*; *Bar Boy*; *Man with Face Creams and Phone Plug*; *Bedroom Boy*; and illustrations for Amitav Ghosh's *Jungle Nama*.

## Principles I'll Carry

Let inherited visual languages become hospitable to people they once excluded. Use color as atmosphere, not decoration. Keep the figure psychologically porous: gestures, rooms, and props can reveal more than exposition. Make intimacy carry tension, and let historical reference serve lived experience.
