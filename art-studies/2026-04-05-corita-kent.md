# Corita Kent — Printmaking / Activist Art

**Date:** 2026-04-05  
**Category:** Printmaking / Activist Art

## Who She Was

Frances Elizabeth Kent (1918–1986), known as Sister Mary Corita Kent, was an American artist, educator, and former Catholic nun based in Los Angeles. She taught art at Immaculate Heart College for over 20 years, becoming head of the art department. In 1968 she left the religious order and moved to Boston, continuing her art practice until her death.

She was arguably the most radical pop artist of the 1960s — and she was a nun. That tension is the entire point.

## Philosophy

Corita's core insight: **the sacred is hiding in the ordinary.** Grocery store packaging, billboard slogans, bread wrappers, song lyrics — she treated the visual noise of consumer America as raw material for spiritual and political awakening. Where Warhol used commercial imagery to comment on emptiness, Corita used it to find fullness. Same visual vocabulary, opposite conclusion.

She believed art should be **democratic** — affordable, reproducible, accessible. Printmaking (serigraphs/silkscreen) was her medium precisely because it allowed mass production. "A very democratic form, since it enables me to produce a quantity of original art for those who cannot afford to purchase high-priced art." Art belongs in workplaces, kitchens, classrooms. Not just galleries.

Her famous "10 Rules for Students and Teachers" (co-authored with John Cage) captures her teaching philosophy. Rule 7: "The only rule is work." Rule 8: "Don't try to create and analyze at the same time. They're different processes." Rule 6: "Nothing is a mistake. There's no win and no fail. There's only make."

## What Makes Her Work Good

1. **Collision of registers.** She layered sacred text over commercial imagery, protest slogans over candy-colored fields, Thoreau quotes over Wonder Bread logos. The friction between registers creates meaning. You can't look at her work passively because the combination forces your brain to reconcile things that don't belong together.

2. **Color as emotion, not decoration.** Her palette is aggressive, joyful, electric. Hot pinks, deep magentas, acid yellows. The color isn't pretty — it's urgent. It demands attention the way a protest sign does.

3. **Text as image.** She dissolved the boundary between reading and seeing. Letters become shapes, words become patterns, slogans become compositions. You experience language visually before you process it intellectually.

4. **Joy as resistance.** In the context of 1960s Vietnam War protests, civil rights struggles, and Vatican II upheaval, her work insisted on beauty and celebration as political acts. Not naive optimism — deliberate, radical joy in the face of suffering. "Damn everything but the circus" (one of her prints, quoting e.e. cummings).

5. **Reproducibility as principle.** The work resists preciousness. It's meant to be printed, distributed, taped to walls. The USPS "LOVE" stamp she designed in 1985 was printed 700 million times. That's not a compromise — it's the fulfillment of her vision.

## Key Works

- **"enriched bread" series (1962–65)** — Serigraphs using Wonder Bread packaging, transforming corporate imagery into something sacramental
- **"the juiciest tomato of all" (1964)** — Del Monte tomato label becomes a meditation on beauty and consumption
- **"stop the bombing" (1967)** — Vietnam War protest print, vivid and direct
- **"LOVE" stamp (1985)** — Designed for USPS, became one of the most reproduced artworks in history
- **"heroes and sheroes" (1969)** — Tribute to activists and thinkers, post-convent period
- **"damn everything but the circus" (1970)** — e.e. cummings quote as manifesto

## Deeper Principles

**Find the sacred in the commercial.** Don't reject the visual environment you live in. Mine it. The grocery store is a cathedral if you look right.

**Accessibility is not a compromise.** Mass production, low cost, and wide distribution aren't betrayals of art — they're the highest expression of what art should do.

**Text and image are one language.** Don't illustrate words or caption images. Let them collide and create a third meaning.

**Joy is not naivety.** Choosing celebration in dark times is the most radical political act available. Despair is easy. Joy requires courage.

**Process over product.** "Nothing is a mistake. There's only make." The act of making IS the point.
