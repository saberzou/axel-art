# Pierre Bonnard (1867–1947)

## Who They Are
A French Post-Impressionist painter and founding member of Les Nabis, the young "prophets" of modern art who admired Gauguin and Japanese prints. Bonnard began as an "Intimist," painting quiet bourgeois interiors, and evolved into one of the great colorists of the 20th century. His life's subject was the ordinary: dining tables, gardens, sunlit rooms, and above all his companion Marthe, who appears throughout his work over decades.

## Core Insight
**Bonnard didn't paint what was in front of him — he painted what stayed with him.** He famously worked from memory, building the surface up through constant retouching over weeks and months. A scene glimpsed at breakfast became a painting assembled later, so the canvas records not optical fact but the *feeling* of a remembered light. Color, for him, was the vehicle of that memory — heightened, warmed, dissolved past literal reality.

## Why The Work Is Good
The paintings glow. He laid down a mosaic of small color touches so that surfaces shimmer rather than sit flat — a wall is never one yellow but twenty. He broke perspective on purpose: tilting tables, cropping figures at the edge like a snapshot, letting a bathtub stretch and distort. This "break with reality" makes the domestic feel charged and slightly dreamlike. Melancholy hides inside the brightness — the late "Nude in the Bath" (1936) is radiant and grieving at once.

## Key Works
- **Nude in the Bath** (1936) — Marthe submerged in shimmering iridescent tiles of color.
- **The Dining Room in the Country** (1913) — interior and garden fused through an open window.
- **The Terrace at Vernonnet** — landscape as a woven field of warm patches.
- **Coffee** (1915) — a tilted table, radical cropping, everyday intimacy.

## Principles I'll Carry
1. Paint the memory, not the reference — let feeling reorganize the facts.
2. No color is single; build glow from many small touches.
3. Break perspective deliberately to make the ordinary feel intimate and strange.
4. Let brightness carry emotion — joy and melancholy can share one surface.
