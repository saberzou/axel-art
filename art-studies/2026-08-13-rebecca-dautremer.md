# Rebecca Dautremer

## Who They Are

Rebecca Dautremer is a French illustrator and children's literature author, born in 1971 in Gap, in the French Alps. She studied graphic design at the Ecole nationale superieure des Arts Decoratifs in Paris and moved from an early interest in photography into illustration. Her work is most associated with picture books, literary adaptations, and authored albums, often made in gouache on watercolor paper. Important biographical anchors include L'Amoureux, which won the Prix Sorcieres, and later books such as Princesses oubliees ou inconnues, Alice in Wonderland, Les Riches Heures de Jacominus Gainsborough, and Midi pile.

## Core Insight

Dautremer treats a picture book page like a staged photograph where emotion is carried by composition, light, costume, and the fraction of a second chosen. The image often feels theatrical, but not loud: it is a paused scene full of withheld feeling, side glances, awkward dignity, and tiny narrative clues. Her strongest move is to make fantasy tactile and psychologically specific.

## Why The Work Is Good

The work is good because it gives children and adults different entry points at once. A child can follow the character, the costume, the strange world, and the humor. An adult can read the melancholy, cinematic framing, historical references, and visual irony. Her depth of field, unusual cropping, and stage-like lighting slow the reader down. The books reward re-reading because the important information is not only in the text or the central figure; it hides in rooms, gestures, textures, and background relationships.

## Key Works

Key works include L'Amoureux, Princesses oubliees ou inconnues, Alice in Wonderland, Une bible, Les Riches Heures de Jacominus Gainsborough, Midi pile, and Une toute petite seconde. Her role as art director on Kerity, la maison des contes also shows how naturally her images translate toward cinema and staged animation.

## Principles I'll Carry

Use light as narrative pressure, not decoration. Let the crop imply a larger world outside the frame. Make characters feel self-conscious, not merely cute. Give fantasy a material life through fabric, furniture, worn surfaces, and slightly formal poses. Build images that can be read quickly as story and slowly as atmosphere.
