# Sophie Blackall

## Who They Are

Sophie Blackall is an Australian-born, Brooklyn-based author and illustrator whose work sits at the center of contemporary children's books. She has illustrated more than fifty books, including the long-running *Ivy and Bean* series, and won the Caldecott Medal twice: in 2016 for illustrating Lindsay Mattick's *Finding Winnie* and in 2019 for writing and illustrating *Hello Lighthouse*. Her career also extends into grown-up books, public projects, and literacy and health initiatives with UNICEF and Save the Children.

## Core Insight

Blackall's deepest strength is making careful looking feel like tenderness. Her pictures do not rush toward spectacle. They notice tools on a shelf, waves around a lighthouse, a child's posture, the emotional weight of an object saved from the past. She turns research and domestic detail into intimacy, so history and daily routine feel breathable rather than remote.

## Why The Work Is Good

Her images balance precision with warmth. The line often feels controlled and observant, while the color carries gentleness and air. In *Finding Winnie*, period detail and archival memory become a family story without losing their factual gravity. In *Hello Lighthouse*, repetition, circular motifs, and the tall vertical format turn a remote building into a living rhythm: weather, work, birth, isolation, rescue, change. Blackall's compositions are especially good at alternating scale. She can pull back to show a world governed by sea and sky, then move close enough for a kettle, logbook, crib, or hand to matter.

## Key Works

Key works include *Ruby's Wish*, her early award-winning collaboration with Shirin Yim Bridges; the *Ivy and Bean* series with Annie Barrows; *Big Red Lollipop* with Rukhsana Khan; *Finding Winnie* with Lindsay Mattick; *Hello Lighthouse*; *If You Come to Earth*; and *Things to Look Forward To*. Together they show her range: comic character observation, historical reconstruction, lyrical place-making, and direct address to a child reader.

## Principles I'll Carry

Let research become feeling, not display. Build a picture around repeated forms and rituals. Use objects as emotional evidence. Keep the palette humane and specific. Allow small rooms, tools, notes, and weather to carry large themes. Make wonder out of attention rather than exaggeration.
