# Roberto Burle Marx (1909–1994)
**Category:** Landscape Design

## Who He Was

Brazilian landscape architect, painter, printmaker, ecologist, naturalist, and musician. Born in São Paulo to a German-Jewish father and Brazilian mother. Grew up in Rio de Janeiro. Ironically, he first encountered Brazilian native plants not in Brazil but in the greenhouses of the Berlin Botanical Garden during a trip to Germany in 1928. That moment of seeing his own country's flora displayed as exotic specimens in a foreign land radicalized his entire practice.

He went on to create over 2,000 gardens and public spaces across Brazil and beyond, collaborating with architects like Oscar Niemeyer and Lúcio Costa on projects including the Ministry of Education and Health in Rio, the Copacabana Beach Promenade, and Brasília's civic landscape.

## Why His Work Is Good

Burle Marx treated landscape as painting. Not metaphorically. He literally approached garden design the way a cubist approaches canvas: plants were color, shape, volume, and texture arranged according to aesthetic laws. His garden plans look like abstract paintings from above, with bold biomorphic shapes, sweeping curves, and massed plantings of single species creating blocks of pure color.

But here's what separates him from mere formalism: he insisted on native plants. Before Burle Marx, Brazilian landscape design was colonial mimicry: European gardens transplanted to the tropics, full of imported species that struggled in local conditions. He reversed the gaze. He mounted expeditions into the Amazon and Atlantic Forest, discovering and cataloging species (over 50 plants bear his name), then deployed them as artistic material. The garden became simultaneously an aesthetic statement and an act of ecological resistance.

His Copacabana promenade pavement (the iconic black and white wave pattern) demonstrates another principle: landscape as civic art. Not decoration applied to a city, but the ground itself becoming a work that millions of people walk across daily without even thinking about it. The best public design disappears into lived experience.

## Core Principles

1. **Painting with living material.** Plants are pigment. Mass them, contrast them, compose them. A garden is a canvas that grows.

2. **The native gaze.** Use what belongs to the place. The most radical design choice is to actually look at where you are instead of importing someone else's aesthetic. Identity lives in indigenous material.

3. **Landscape as art, not decoration.** A garden is not ornamentation for architecture. It is an autonomous artwork with its own logic, its own rhythm, its own emotional register.

4. **Time as medium.** Unlike painting or sculpture, landscape design includes growth, decay, seasonal change, and succession. The work is never finished. It is always becoming.

5. **Ecology as aesthetics.** Biodiversity isn't a constraint on beauty. It IS beauty. The richest compositions come from understanding how species relate, compete, and coexist.

6. **Public space as collective artwork.** His best work is not private gardens for the wealthy but promenades, parks, and plazas where the art is the ground you stand on, the canopy above you, the texture underfoot.

## Key Works

- **Copacabana Beach Promenade** (1970) — The iconic wave-patterned mosaic pavement. Abstract art at urban scale, walked on by millions.
- **Ministry of Education and Health Rooftop Garden, Rio** (1938) — One of the first modernist rooftop gardens, with Niemeyer. Announced his approach to the world.
- **Ibirapuera Park, São Paulo** (1954) — Landscape for Niemeyer's pavilions. Biomorphic plant beds as abstract composition.
- **Sítio Roberto Burle Marx** (his personal estate, now UNESCO World Heritage) — A living laboratory of 3,500+ species. The garden as autobiography.
- **Flamengo Park, Rio** (1965) — 1.2 million square meters of reclaimed waterfront. Landscape as urban infrastructure.
- **Edmundo Cavanelas Residence Garden** (1954) — Collaboration with Niemeyer. Sinuous garden beds flowing around the house like rivers.

## What Endures

Burle Marx proved that looking at your own place, really looking, is the most avant-garde thing you can do. Every imported aesthetic is someone else's answer to someone else's question. The radical move is to ask your own question using the material that's already under your feet. His gardens age well because they were designed WITH time, not against it. They grow into themselves rather than deteriorating from a perfect moment. That's the deepest lesson: make work that gets better by being alive.
