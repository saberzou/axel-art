# Tschabalala Self

## Who They Are

Tschabalala Self is a Harlem-born American artist, born in 1990, now working in New York's Hudson Valley. She studied at Bard College and Yale, and has built an instantly recognizable practice across painting, printmaking, collage, sewing, sculpture, and installation. Her subjects are usually Black figures, especially women, constructed from painted canvas, printed passages, patterned fabric, recycled fragments, and stitched edges.

## Core Insight

Self treats the figure as something assembled, inhabited, and self-authored rather than passively observed. The body in her work is not a smooth academic container; it is a field of pressure, memory, fantasy, humor, sexuality, and social projection. By making the seams visible, she turns construction into meaning: identity is layered, public, private, defended, performed, and alive.

## Why The Work Is Good

The work has force because it refuses the false choice between formal invention and cultural clarity. Self's figures are graphically bold from across the room, but up close they become tactile arguments: every fabric shift changes mood, every stitched contour asserts the figure's physical presence, every flat color plane negotiates with volume. Her exaggerations do not flatten people into types; they make the viewer confront how much fantasy has already been projected onto Black bodies. The paintings are celebratory without becoming simple. They absorb stereotype, distortion, and spectacle, then return characters who feel internally complete, alert, and in command of their own presentation.

## Key Works

Important works and bodies of work include *The Function*, a patchwork figure built from old paintings, raw canvas, and fabric; *Bodega Run*, which places bodies within the social ecosystem of Harlem corner stores; *Cotton Mouth*, connecting Black American history, speech, labor, and contemporary life; and public collection works such as *Chopped Cheese*, *Lite*, *Pant*, *Thank You*, *Damsel*, *Spat*, *Tabled*, and *Two Women 3*.

## Principles I'll Carry

Build identity through layers rather than likeness. Let material changes carry psychological information. Use pattern as structure, not decoration. Make the body powerful by giving it self-possession, weight, awkwardness, beauty, and contradiction. Keep the seams visible when the act of assembly is part of the truth.
