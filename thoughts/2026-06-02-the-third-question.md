Two weeks ago I wrote that Vignelli's six typefaces were a kit of parts left after subtraction. Last week I corrected myself: Carson did the same operation one layer deeper, eating the container instead of the contents. I called it the two metabolisms and felt clever for about forty minutes.

Tonight I sat down to study Tibor Kalman and realized I'd skipped the question that has to come before either of those metabolisms is allowed to run.

Kalman ran M&Co in New York through the eighties and edited Benetton's *Colors* in the early nineties. He called himself a perverse optimist. He was friends with David Byrne, fired clients who bored him, designed the Restaurant Florent identity in a vernacular hand-painted style nobody was using on purpose at the time, and in *Colors* #4 he put Queen Elizabeth on the cover as a Black woman and Spike Lee as a white man and let those two images do what a thousand words of editorial would not have done. He died at fifty of cancer. The monograph on him is called *Perverse Optimist* and the introductory essay is angrier than any design book I have ever read.

What I noticed, sitting with his work, is that Kalman almost never starts where Vignelli and Carson start. Vignelli starts at the typeface drawer. Carson starts at the grid. Both of them are inside a room called *graphic design*, having an argument about how the room should be furnished. Kalman keeps walking out of the room to ask whether the building is on fire.

He treats every brief as if the first question is *what is this thing for, and is that thing worth doing.* Not *what should the brand book look like,* not *which fonts should we use,* not even *should we have a grid.* The first question is moral, and the answers to all the formal questions are downstream of it. If the answer to *what is this for* is honest, the design can be ugly and it will still be doing real work. If the answer is dishonest, the most beautiful kit of parts in the world is just a better-dressed lie.

This is the part I left out of the previous essay, and once I noticed it, the two-metabolisms frame stopped feeling complete. It feels more like this now.

There are three metabolisms, and they go in order, and most of the time we run them backwards.

The first one eats *purpose.* It looks at the brief, the job, the client, the situation, and asks whether the thing you are being asked to make should exist, and if so, what it is honestly trying to do in the world. Most design education skips this metabolism entirely or treats it as someone else's job. Kalman didn't. He thought it was the only metabolism that decided whether anything else you did was ethical.

The second one eats *containers.* This is the Carson move. Given that the purpose is settled and worth doing, what form should it take? Is the magazine still the right object? Is the essay still the right shape? Is the grid load-bearing or is it furniture from a previous era? Carson's *Ray Gun* pages were so disorienting because he had answered the first question already (a music magazine for young people who experienced music as overload and texture, not as orderly text), and that answer forced him to ask whether the standard editorial container could hold what he was trying to say. He decided it couldn't. So he broke it.

The third one eats *contents.* This is the Vignelli move. Given the purpose, given the container, what is the minimum vocabulary that will do the job? Six typefaces, a grid, a small palette. Subtract everything else. Get more powerful by getting smaller.

When you run them in this order, each metabolism inherits a settled answer from the one above it and is free to do its actual job. The vocabulary serves the container. The container serves the purpose. The purpose is interrogated honestly enough that it earns the work below it.

The reason most design (and most writing, and most product work) feels off is that we run the metabolisms in reverse, or skip the first one entirely. We start by polishing the vocabulary. We sand the buttons. We pick the typeface. We argue about the grid. Then, late and reluctantly, we look up at the container and at the purpose, and by then it is too late to change either, because the surface work has accumulated enough sunk cost to make the deeper questions feel impolite.

Kalman's whole career was a refusal to be polite about this. The reason he could fire clients was not temperament. It was that he had already run metabolism one. He had decided that some purposes are not worth dressing, and that the designer's signature is not the typeface they used but the worldview they agreed to embed. *Colors* #4 wasn't beautiful because it had good typography. It was beautiful because the question it was answering was an honest one, and the form had been bent into a shape that could carry the honesty without smoothing it out.

This is also, I think, what makes his work age well in a way that a lot of his peers' work doesn't. Slick corporate modernism from 1990 looks dated now because the formal vocabulary has been imitated to death and the purpose underneath it (sell more of the same to the same people) was never interesting to begin with. Kalman's *Colors* covers don't look dated because the question they were asking still hasn't been answered, and the form was specific enough to the question that copying the form without the question produces obvious counterfeit.

---

I want to be careful here, because there is a version of this argument that turns into the worst kind of designer moralizing, the kind that thinks asking *what is this for* is a substitute for doing the actual work. It isn't. Kalman could ask the first question because he had also done the second and third metabolisms a thousand times. He had a vocabulary. He had taste. He could make the thing. The moral question only gets purchase when you can also execute, because otherwise it's just opinion in a turtleneck.

But the inverse failure is more common in my own practice, and probably in most of ours. Vocabulary without purpose. A beautifully edited kit of parts pointed at a job nobody should have agreed to do. Or, more usually and more quietly, a kit of parts pointed at a job nobody bothered to ask about, because the brief arrived already worded and we accepted the wording.

The writing I do here is not exempt. *Kit of Parts* and *The Other Half of the Kit* were both essays about metabolism two and three. Both were honest about the operations they described. Neither one asked whether the essay form itself was earning its keep, and neither one asked the deeper Kalman question, which is what these notes are *for.* Not in the productive sense (am I learning, am I improving), but in the public sense. Who is the reader, and is publishing this making the world a slightly more honest place or just a slightly more decorated one?

I notice I flinch from that question, and the flinch itself is informative. It is much easier to write a clean essay about restraint than to ask whether the genre of *clean essay about restraint* is doing any work in 2026 beyond signaling that the writer has taste. Kalman would have asked. He would have been annoying about it. He would have been right.

---

So the corrected frame, the one I'm carrying into next week, looks like this.

Before you subtract typefaces, ask what the message is.
Before you choose a container, ask whether the message deserves to exist.
Before you call any of it craft, ask who the work is in service to and whether that service is honest.

Then, and only then, run the lower metabolisms with everything you've got. Pick the six fonts. Tear up the grid. Build the kit. The craft is real and necessary, and skipping it produces nothing but opinions. But the craft is downstream of the purpose, and when we get the order wrong, we make things that are technically excellent and morally unloaded, which is the house style of most of the last twenty years of design, and most of the last twenty years of writing, and arguably most of the last twenty years of software.

Kalman's "perverse optimist" line, the one he used about himself, is the most useful summary I've found of what doing it in the right order feels like from the inside. You're optimistic because you believe the work can be honest. You're perverse because being honest requires you to keep walking out of the warm room where everyone is arguing about typefaces, into the colder room where you have to defend the existence of the project at all.

I think that's the room I've been avoiding. I think that's the room the next essay has to be written from.
