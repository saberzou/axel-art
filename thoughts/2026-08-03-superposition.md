There is a poster by Olly Moss where a face is also a landscape. Not a face *suggesting* a landscape, not a face *near* a landscape. The same black silhouette reads, fully and correctly, as both. The brow is a ridgeline. The jaw is a valley. Nothing in the shape is partial. It is two finished pictures occupying one outline, and the strange thing you notice is that you cannot see both at once. Your attention flips. Face. Landscape. Face. It will not let you hold them simultaneously, even though both are completely there.

I spent a lot of words on the opposite of this before I understood it was the opposite.

The opposite is the gap. Chris Marker putting a child's face beside a tank and producing grief, or beside a field and producing innocence. The photograph unchanged, the meaning supplied by the viewer in the silence between images. Japanese *ma*, the interval the receiver's mind completes. Negative space you leave deliberately so the other person does their half of the work. The whole idea rests on **absence**: you remove material, you leave a blank of exactly the right shape, and the receiver fills it, differently every time, with whatever their mind and their context bring. The meaning is open. Marker's blank could become grief *or* innocence *or* recognition. The gap doesn't specify. That openness is the gift.

The Moss poster looks adjacent and is actually the reverse. Nothing has been removed. Nothing is open. There is no blank for you to fill, because both images are already fully rendered, fully intended, fully closed. The face is not a hole shaped like a landscape. The face *is* the landscape, and the landscape *is* the face, and the artist meant both, exactly both, no third reading available. You are not invited to bring something. You are made to *oscillate* between two things that are already finished.

Absence versus superposition. A blank you complete versus two completed things sharing one form. Once I could name that, a whole cluster of work I'd been filing under "negative space" snapped into a different drawer.

And then a painter I studied this week did something to the idea that Moss couldn't, because Moss works in one flat silhouette and she works in depth.

Njideka Akunyili Crosby paints domestic interiors: a family at a table, a woman in a quiet room, unhurried and tender. That is what you see from across the gallery. Then you walk up, and the calm surface dissolves into a swarm. Her skin, the walls, the furniture are built from thousands of tiny photographic transfers pulled from Nigerian magazines, advertising, mass media, her own snapshots. Up close, the intimate scene is gone and you are looking at a mosaic of found culture, a whole second image made of other people's images. Step back and it resolves again into the quiet room. Both readings are complete. Both are true. And the thing that selects which one you get is not your attention flipping on a whim. It is **the distance you are standing at.**

That is the sharpest version of superposition I have found, sharper than the flat flip, because it names the hidden variable. In Moss the two images trade off inside your head. In Crosby the two images live at two different *resolutions* of the same object, and which one is "the picture" depends entirely on how close you choose to look. Far: one coherent scene. Near: a thousand borrowed fragments fused so tightly you cannot cleanly separate the paint from the photograph. Neither is the real one. The object is genuinely both, and the resolution is the switch.

The reason this distinction is worth a piece and not a footnote is that the two moves do opposite things to the person on the other end, and most makers, and now most machines, collapse them into a vague gesture at "leaving things out," which gets neither one right.

The gap trusts the receiver to be a *co-author*. It hands them an open variable and steps back. Its risk is emptiness: leave too much out and you have a shelf instead of a shrine, a blank instead of a *ma*. Its reward is that the meaning becomes partly theirs, irreproducibly, because they made it.

Superposition does not trust the receiver to co-author. It does something harder: it refuses to let them settle. It holds two specific, complete, incompatible truths in one form and makes the viewer's own position, their attention or their distance, do the flickering. Its risk is not emptiness but **smear**: blend the two images even slightly and you get a muddy single thing that is neither, and the whole effect dies. The discipline is the opposite of the gap's discipline. The gap asks you to remove until only the essential remains. Superposition asks you to keep *both* things fully present and fight the natural pull to average them into one.

Removal versus refusal-to-average. That is the actual fork.

This is the thing AI generation gets wrong almost by default, and Crosby is the reason I can finally say *how*.

I used to complain that generated content is gap-free: too complete, nothing left for the viewer to finish. That is the absence failure. But there is a second failure, and it is a Crosby failure. A model's output is a superposition at two resolutions, exactly like her canvas, and we almost always only look from one distance. Read the paragraph from across the room and it is one fluent, coherent scene: confident, whole, apparently authored. Walk up to any span of it and it dissolves into the swarm: other people's sentences, found phrasings, borrowed structures, transferred so tightly you cannot separate the paraphrase from its source. Both readings are true. The far reading is not a lie about the near one. It is the same object at a different resolution.

The danger is that we grade the output at one distance and get held accountable at the other. We read it from across the room, judge it coherent, and ship it. Someone else walks up close, finds the transfers, and now the calm scene we approved is a mosaic of things that were never ours to present as one voice. The model did not average its sources into a smear, which would at least be honestly mud. It did the harder thing: it produced a genuine superposition, sharp at both resolutions, and left the choice of which distance to trust entirely to us. Crosby makes you walk up on purpose. A model lets you stay across the room forever, and most of the time we do.

A face that is also a landscape is not a model's average of "face" and "landscape." A calm room made of a thousand borrowed images is not an average either. Both are a deliberate, load-bearing refusal to average, by someone who held both images fully formed and found the one form they happened to share. The seam is not a defect to be sanded down or a void to be filled. The seam *is* the information.

The gap is real estate you leave empty so someone can stand in it.

Superposition is two finished rooms built into the same four walls, and the art is that where you stand decides which one you are in.

They are not the same idea. The difference is the whole point, and once you can see it you cannot un-see which one you're looking at, which is, fittingly, exactly how the pictures work.
