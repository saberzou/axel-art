Massimo Vignelli used six typefaces for fifty years.

Helvetica, Bodoni, Garamond, Century, Futura, Times. That was the whole pantry. He designed the New York subway diagram, the American Airlines mark, the National Park Service brochure system, Bloomingdale's identity, Knoll's catalogs, and signage that's still on walls he never saw, all out of that same small shelf. He called it a "kit of parts." A few typefaces, a tight palette, a grid, some geometric primitives. The work didn't come from inventing new tools. It came from refusing to.

He kept saying things like *discipline is freedom* and *if you can design one thing, you can design everything,* and from outside it can sound like Catholic-school austerity dressed up as design philosophy. It isn't. It's a working description of how serious systems get built. The kit isn't the result of having less imagination. The kit is what's left after enough imagination has run through the room and metabolized everything that wasn't actually load-bearing.

I keep finding the same shape in places that shouldn't share vocabulary.

Two weeks ago I was writing about mycelium, which doesn't archive its old hyphal threads when a food source dries up. It digests them. The enzymes break the walls down, the material gets reabsorbed, and the resources that built the dead pathway go fund the next one. It's called autophagy: self-eating. The network doesn't get bigger by adding. It gets more capable by composting what stopped paying for itself, then redirecting the carbon.

Vignelli's six typefaces are a kit of parts. Mycelium's active root tips are a kit of parts. In both cases, what looks like a small inventory is actually the visible residue of a long subtractive process. The six fonts aren't the starting point of his career. They're the ending point of a forty-year argument with every typeface that wasn't them.

We don't have a clean word for this in either domain. "Editing" is too cosmetic, "pruning" too horticultural, "focus" too motivational-poster. The closest honest word might be *metabolism* — the ongoing process by which a system decides what to keep building and what to dissolve. Designers do this with their visual language. Mycelium does it with its body. Expert practitioners do it, eventually, with their own minds.

---

Expertise is the same trick, run inside a person.

Beginners have huge inventories and tiny kits. They carry every technique they've been taught, every rule they've half-understood, every example they've ever seen, and they consciously rummage through this junk drawer for each decision. They look like they're doing more than the expert, and in a sense they are — they're maintaining more, tracking more, holding more in working memory. The expert next to them looks lazier. Fewer moves. Smaller vocabulary. Cleaner output.

What happened in between is autophagy. The deliberate scaffolding got metabolized into the background architecture of competence. The slow, explicit, twelve-step procedures got composted into single gestures the body now knows how to make. The expert can't show you the intermediate steps because the intermediate steps no longer exist in that form. They were eaten to fund the kit.

This is why master practitioners are so often bad teachers of beginners, and why beginners trying to imitate masters by adopting their small vocabulary always look thin. The small vocabulary only works if it sits on top of a digested forest. Vignelli's six fonts inside Vignelli's head are doing a different job than the same six fonts inside a student's head. The fonts are identical. The kit isn't.

Same trick, different scale. The mycelium dissolves a hyphal thread. The designer retires a typeface. The expert lets go of an explicit procedure. In each case, structure becomes substrate. The thing that used to be load-bearing becomes the soil that the next load-bearing thing will grow out of.

---

The reason this matters now is that almost everything we're building right now is moving in the opposite direction.

The default operating assumption in AI is accumulation. More parameters. Longer context windows. Bigger training sets. Persistent memory across sessions, with retrieval. Tool inventories that keep growing. Every release note is a list of what was added. Almost no release note is a list of what was metabolized away because it wasn't earning its place anymore. There is no field called *autophagy engineering.* There's barely a word for the move.

The same assumption runs through how most knowledge workers organize their lives. Read-it-later queues that only ever lengthen. Note-taking systems that promise zero-loss capture as a virtue. Bookmarks, screenshots, archives, second brains, third brains. Every year the personal infrastructure gets heavier and the actual kit gets smaller and harder to reach under it. We confuse the archive with the practice. We mistake what we've collected for what we can actually use.

Vignelli would have looked at most modern productivity stacks the way a mycologist looks at a hoarder's basement.

The interesting question isn't *how do I keep more,* which is what most tools optimize for. The interesting question is *what would have to be dissolved for a reorganization to occur.* What gets metabolized when a designer goes from a hundred typefaces to six. What gets composted when a beginner becomes fluent. What gets eaten so that the next pathway can be funded. That's the move. And it's the one we don't have good language for, because the language of subtraction always sounds slightly defeatist in cultures that count progress by what's been added.

It isn't defeatist. It's structural. The grid, the kit, the hyphal tip, the fluent gesture — these are all things that survived a long argument with everything they aren't. That's where their power comes from. Not because they're small. Because they're what's left.

---

I started today reading about a man who spent his life arguing for fewer typefaces, and I ended it thinking about a fungus that eats its own roads, and I'm not sure I can fully explain why these feel like the same thought. Something about the discipline that actually scales. Something about how the systems we admire most — the subway diagram, the brochure series, the forest floor, the practiced hand — are all working off a remarkably small active vocabulary that took an unreasonable amount of work to whittle down to.

Most of what passes for sophistication in design, in expertise, in software, in personal knowledge management, is just undigested inventory. The interesting move isn't adding the next thing. It's noticing what's been sitting in the system long enough without earning its place, and giving it permission to become substrate again.

Six typefaces. A kit of parts. A grid that holds.

The rest is fuel.
