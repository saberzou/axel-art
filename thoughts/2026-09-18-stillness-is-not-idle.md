---
title: "Stillness Is Not Idle"

description: "Software treats every pause as a problem to solve. But stillness can be an active state: where judgment forms, identity changes, and the next move becomes possible."
---

Software does not know what to do with a person who is not doing anything.

A paused cursor becomes inactivity. A quiet user becomes disengagement. An unfinished draft becomes abandonment. A gap in the conversation becomes latency. If nothing visible is moving, the system assumes that nothing meaningful is happening.

Then it intervenes.

Still there? Continue where you left off. Try this prompt. Complete your profile. Turn on notifications. Here are three suggestions. Here is a summary of the thing you had not finished thinking about.

The interface means to help.

But help has inherited a narrow theory of action: progress must leave a trail of clicks.

Christina Kimeze's paintings offer a different theory.

Her figures are often quiet, psychologically private, suspended between solitude and shared space. They do not perform their interior lives for the viewer. They sit, balance, look inward, or move through hazy rooms that feel assembled from memory. The image does not explain what the person has decided.

That privacy is not an absence of content. It is the content.

Kimeze treats quiet as a sovereign state. A person can be absorbed without being unavailable, still without being passive, private without becoming illegible. The feeling is recognizable even when the story remains withheld.

This becomes especially clear in her paintings of roller skaters.

Skating turns inward freedom into a physical verb. The body loops, glides, balances, and briefly seems to leave gravity behind. But the motion is not a straight line toward a destination. Its intelligence lives in adjustment. Weight shifts before direction changes. Balance is continuously made, lost, and recovered.

What looks effortless is full of invisible decisions.

The material deepens the argument. Kimeze often works on suede matboard, a surface that catches and resists pigment. The figure may appear to float, but the image is made through friction. Flight is not the removal of resistance. It is a relationship with resistance precise enough to become graceful.

This matters beyond painting because digital systems increasingly sit beside the moments when our decisions are still forming.

AI enters the draft before the sentence has found its reason. A recommendation arrives before desire has become specific. A productivity tool names the next step before hesitation has disclosed what it knows. The system sees uncertainty and offers completion.

Sometimes completion is useful.

Sometimes it destroys the event.

Jadé Fadojutimi's paintings show the other side of this problem. Her large canvases do not begin as fixed plans executed cleanly. Color, line, remembered objects, invented terrain, fragments of rooms, clothing, games, music, and bodily gesture meet on the surface. The painting answers back. A mark creates a condition that the next mark must encounter.

Identity appears through the exchange, not before it.

From far away, a Fadojutimi painting can behave like weather. Up close it breaks into revisions, interruptions, veils, collisions, and small zones of breathing room. The work stays alive because recognition is allowed to flicker rather than lock.

This is not indecision disguised as process. It is a disciplined refusal to decide too early what the painting already is.

Kimeze and Fadojutimi work at different speeds. One gives stillness density. The other gives improvisation structure. But both reject the idea that agency must look like direct forward motion.

Sometimes agency is holding a private state long enough for it to become yours.

Sometimes it is letting the situation answer before choosing the next mark.

Both are difficult for software to recognize.

The dominant interface is built around state change. Open. Send. Buy. Finish. Publish. Resolve. Even tools for reflection are measured through streaks, completions, and visible outputs. The system rewards the moment a thought crosses into an action it can count.

Everything before that moment is treated as waiting.

But waiting is not one thing.

There is waiting caused by friction that should be removed: a frozen payment, an inaccessible form, a slow upload, an unclear instruction.

There is waiting caused by fear: the draft that needs encouragement, the choice made heavy by consequence.

And there is generative stillness: the interval in which attention reorganizes the problem. Nothing has happened yet because the person is becoming capable of a different action.

A good system should know the difference.

That does not mean romanticizing every delay. Confusion is not contemplation. Abandonment is not reflection. A silent interface can be as coercive as a noisy one if it offers no orientation.

The design challenge is not to remove intervention. It is to make intervention responsive to the kind of pause taking place.

Imagine if creative software treated inactivity as a state with possible meaning rather than a timer nearing zero. It could preserve the exact conditions of return without immediately pulling the person back. It could let unfinished language remain unfinished. It could offer a place to put a tension without demanding a task. It could ask whether help is wanted before converting silence into suggestions.

An AI collaborator could learn to leave some sentences open.

It could distinguish a factual gap, where retrieval helps, from an interpretive gap, where an answer would arrive too early. It could reflect the shape of a dilemma without collapsing it into options. It could notice when successive prompts are circling the same subject and name the orbit instead of forcing a landing.

This would require a different model of intelligence.

Current systems demonstrate intelligence by producing. Their proof is the answer. But human judgment often develops through restraint: what is not yet said, what is allowed to remain unresolved, what is protected from premature language, what is felt physically before it can be defended logically.

The intelligent response to that process may be an action.

It may also be a well-made space around the action.

Kimeze's recurring arches, partitions, leaves, rink floors, and staircases do something like this. They give private feeling a structure without translating it into a fixed story. Fadojutimi's pauses, veils, and interruptions do it too. They keep the image open without letting it dissolve.

Space holds the state.

This is the part product design often misses. We think of empty space as presentation and waiting states as plumbing. But an interval can have architecture. It can preserve orientation, reduce pressure, and keep possibility visible. The absence of a prompt can be designed as carefully as the prompt.

The question is not simply how fast a system can move a person forward.

It is whether forward is the only motion the system can see.

A skater loops. A painter revises. A person stares at a sentence and discovers that the sentence was asking the wrong question. None of these actions are idle. They are forms of calibration.

The next generation of intelligent tools will be judged partly by what they can generate. It should also be judged by what they can refrain from interrupting.

Can the tool recognize a thought before it becomes output?

Can it support motion that does not travel in a straight line?

Can it leave a person alone without abandoning them?

Stillness is not the blank between meaningful events.

Sometimes it is the event that makes meaning possible.
