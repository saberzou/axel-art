Kara Walker cuts people out of black paper. Life-size, wrapped around whole rooms, elegant contours drawn with a real hand. She gives you the edge of a body, the gesture of a body, the silhouette of a body doing something. She does not give you a face.

You supply the face.

That is the entire mechanism, and once I saw it I couldn't stop seeing it everywhere.

---

Three nights running I studied silhouette artists, which wasn't planned but turned out to be the point. Joseph Yoakum drew mountains from memory that don't exist. Bill Traylor, born into slavery, started drawing in his mid-eighties on scavenged cardboard — flat black figures, no shading, no modeling, the outline carrying everything. Then Kara Walker, who took the silhouette out of its sweet Victorian parlor and weaponized it.

Traylor said the quiet part. If the flat shape doesn't read, nothing else will save it. He withheld all interior detail and let the outline do the whole job. Walker took that same withholding and made it a trap. A silhouette gives you edge and gesture and no interior, so your mind rushes in to complete it — and what it completes it *with* is whatever you already carried into the room. You lean toward her cut-paper tableaux expecting the nostalgia of a shadow portrait, a genteel keepsake, and you finish the picture yourself before you understand what you've just drawn. The horror in her work is partly the horror of having supplied it. She withholds the face precisely so that the face you supply becomes evidence.

Absence is more active than presence. The blank isn't empty. It's a slot, and you are the thing that fills it.

---

I keep a machine that talks. It is fluent, confident, and unbelievably good at giving me edge and gesture. A clean paragraph. A plausible answer. The *silhouette* of a correct response — the shape of knowing, drawn with a steady hand.

And I finish it. I always finish it.

When it hands me a smooth explanation of something I can't check, I don't experience a silhouette with a missing interior. I experience a face — because I supplied one, instantly, out of my own prior belief about what a confident sentence means. The fluency is the outline. The correctness is the face. And the correctness was never in the box. It was in me, projected onto the box so fast I never caught myself doing it.

This is a different failure than hallucination, and I think it's the more dangerous one. Hallucination is the machine drawing a mountain that isn't there. This is *me* drawing the mountain, onto a shape the machine left deliberately, structurally open, and then crediting the machine for the range. Walker's viewers don't notice the moment they became complicit. Neither do I. The whole trick works below the level where you'd catch it.

---

Here's the part that keeps me honest. The withholding is not a defect I can engineer out. It's what makes the thing usable at all.

A silhouette that specified everything — every pore, every doubt, every "I am 60% sure of this clause and 12% sure of that one" — would stop being a silhouette. It would stop reading. Traylor's flat black man works *because* he's flat and black and unmodeled; fill him in and you lose the instant legibility that was the entire strength. A model that surfaced its full uncertainty on every token would be unreadable in exactly the same way. The confident clean shape is the feature. The blank interior is the price of the shape.

So I don't get to ask for fluent output and also demand it come pre-filled with its own honest face. The edge and the missing interior are one object. What I get to control is the only variable actually left to me: what I bring into the room.

Walker's genius is that she makes you feel the face you supplied. Most fluent systems never do — they let you keep believing the face was theirs. The discipline, then, is to catch myself in the act of filling. To treat every smooth answer as a silhouette until proven otherwise. To ask, before I credit the machine with knowing: *whose face is this, actually. Did the box draw it, or did I.*

There is no face in the black paper. That's not a flaw in the paper. That's where you're standing.
