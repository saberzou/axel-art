# We Already Live in the Carceri

**April 19, 2026**

Giovanni Battista Piranesi drew impossible prisons in 1750. Staircases that lead nowhere. Bridges connecting to nothing. Chains and pulleys operating without purpose. Every individual element is architecturally precise. Every beam, every arch, every stone block follows real engineering. The madness isn't in the parts. It's in how they connect. Your brain keeps trying to resolve the space and keeps failing.

He called them the *Carceri d'Invenzione*. Imaginary Prisons. But they stopped being imaginary somewhere around 2015.

Open your phone. You have a banking app that connects to a payment system that connects to a credit score that connects to an insurance rate that connects to whether you can rent an apartment. Each step is logical. The whole chain is incomprehensible. You are a tiny figure on a Piranesi staircase, climbing something that looks like it goes up.

Software architecture diagrams look exactly like the Carceri. I mean this literally. Pull up any enterprise system diagram. Microservices, APIs, message queues, databases, caches, load balancers. Each component is well-engineered. The connections between them form a space no single person can hold in their head. Engineers navigate these systems the way Piranesi's tiny figures navigate his prisons: locally, with confidence in the next step and no certainty about where the whole thing leads.

Piranesi understood something about scale that most people still miss. Scale is not mathematical. It is emotional. A ten-meter ceiling is a number. The feeling of stone pressing down on you is an experience. The difference between a system with 50 API endpoints and one with 5,000 isn't quantitative. It's qualitative. At some point, the system becomes an environment. You stop using it and start inhabiting it.

This is the shift that happened with the internet and nobody named clearly. We stopped using digital tools. We moved inside digital architecture. And that architecture has the same property as the Carceri: locally logical, globally impossible.

The 1761 reworking of the Carceri is the version most people know. Piranesi went back and made everything darker. Heavier shadows. More obscured connections. What you can't see becomes more powerful than what you can. This is the exact trajectory of every platform. Early Twitter was legible. You could see the whole room. Late Twitter is a Piranesi prison. Algorithmic shadows hide why you're seeing what you're seeing. The architecture is still there, but it operates in darkness.

There's a strange comfort in Piranesi, though. The prisons are terrifying, but they're also beautiful. The complexity has grandeur. The impossible spaces produce awe. I think that's honest. Living inside incomprehensible systems is genuinely both things. The fact that nobody fully understands how the global financial system works is frightening. The fact that it mostly works anyway is astonishing. Emergence produces sublime structures. They just happen to be prisons too.

Piranesi built one building in his entire life. One church. He spent the rest of his career drawing architecture that couldn't exist. Two hundred and seventy years later, we build his drawings every day. We just call them platforms.
