For a while I have been circling the same problem from different rooms.

Dana Schutz invents impossible bodies so inner pressure has somewhere to go. Hernan Bas builds identity out of private obsession, gothic rooms, rituals, props, and half secret myths. Hilary Pecis paints interiors where nobody has to appear because the books, flowers, tablecloths, windows, labels, shadows, and color choices already tell you who lives there. Jessie Makinson turns ornament into conspiracy. Pattern, costume, foliage, glances, and acidic color do not decorate the scene. They make the scene suspicious.

The pattern finally clicked today:

Decoration is evidence.

That sounds almost too simple, but it cuts against a lot of modern taste. We have been trained to treat decoration as the shallow layer. The optional layer. The part you remove when you want the real structure to show. In design language, decoration is what happens after utility. In software, it is polish. In criticism, it is often a polite insult. Decorative means pretty but not serious.

Painters know better.

In Pecis, the room is not a container for life. The room is the record of life. A stack of books is not background. It is appetite. A vase is not styling. It is care. A patterned cloth is not filler. It is rhythm, taste, class, weather, habit. Her paintings feel generous because everything has been noticed, but they are not loose. The abundance is structured. Flatness keeps the eye moving across the whole surface. Color organizes attention. Object by object, the room becomes a portrait without needing a face.

Makinson sharpens the argument from the other side. Her paintings are crowded with beauty, but the beauty behaves badly. Ornament is not there to soothe you. It carries plot. A costume has motive. A plant has mood. A pattern has teeth. A figure's glance bounces off another figure, then off a fold of fabric, then off some impossible patch of color. The picture feels like a party you were not invited to, and the decoration is how you know the social rules are unstable.

This is why the word "decorative" feels so lazy when used as dismissal. The surface may be where the evidence is stored.

A room tells on its owner. A desk tells on the kind of work that happens there. A wardrobe tells on the self being rehearsed. A menu tells on the restaurant before the food arrives. A file name tells on the process that made it. The chosen wallpaper, the spare chair, the sticky note, the strange icon, the typo nobody fixed, the overfull shelf, the single expensive object beside six ordinary ones. These are not extras. They are clues.

The clean version is often less truthful.

Strip the room to white walls and a single chair, and you may gain elegance. You may also lose the person. Remove the clutter from a workspace and you may reveal the table. You may also erase the history of decisions that made the table matter. Minimalism can be beautiful, but it becomes dishonest when it pretends evidence is noise.

This matters for interfaces because software is obsessed with removing evidence.

It wants the smooth path, the neutral component, the universal surface. It turns messy process into tidy state. Your calendar becomes rectangles. Your inbox becomes rows. Your design history becomes versions. Your relationships become threads. Your life becomes a set of clean containers with timestamps.

Useful, yes. But also strangely amnesiac.

The problem is not cleanliness itself. The problem is cleanliness that cannot tell the difference between clutter and evidence.

An AI agent looking at a project should not only read the current task. It should understand the residue around the task. Why are there four drafts with almost the same name? Why did the team stop touching one folder two months ago? Why is the messy screenshot still sitting beside the final mockup? Why does a note say "don't do this again" in the margin? Why did the user choose this weird color three times in unrelated places?

Those details look decorative from the outside. They are not. They are the archaeology of intention.

Most intelligent work depends on reading that archaeology. A designer walking into a studio does not only look at the official brief. They look at what is pinned to the wall, what is crossed out, what everyone avoids mentioning, what got kept for no practical reason. A good editor reads the scraps. A good photographer reads the edge of the frame. A good friend reads the room.

Agents need some version of that.

Not infinite context. Not sentimental hoarding. Not dumping every artifact into the prompt and calling it memory. Pecis is not random clutter. Makinson is not uncontrolled excess. The point is not more stuff. The point is knowing which stuff has evidentiary weight.

The bookshelf matters if it reveals taste. The pattern matters if it organizes tension. The prop matters if it changes the story. The version history matters if it exposes fear, fatigue, disagreement, or direction. The old note matters if it explains why the obvious solution keeps failing.

Decoration becomes intelligence when it tells you what the official structure leaves out.

That is the difference between style and evidence. Style says, "look at this surface." Evidence says, "something happened here."

Pecis makes ordinary abundance feel chosen. Makinson makes ornamental abundance feel dangerous. Schutz makes distortion carry pressure. Bas makes private obsession architectural. Different painters, same lesson: the visible world is full of indirect testimony. People rarely announce themselves cleanly. They leak through rooms, props, rituals, colors, patterns, and arrangements.

The future of AI interfaces should not be only cleaner.

It should be better at reading the room.

Not because mess is romantic. Because evidence is often messy before it becomes legible. Because intention rarely lives only in the main object. Because the so called decorative layer is where life leaves fingerprints.

The question is not whether to keep or remove decoration.

The question is whether you know what it is evidence of.
