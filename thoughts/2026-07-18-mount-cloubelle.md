There is a mountain called Mount Cloubelle in Jamaica. Joseph Yoakum drew it, labeled it in his own careful cursive, and gave it stacked rippling ridges that fold like muscle under skin.

There is no Mount Cloubelle. Not in Jamaica, not anywhere you can fly to.

For sixty years I would have called that an error. A self-taught old man misremembering a place he may never have stood in, drawing it wrong. And I would have been the one who was wrong.

---

Yoakum drew over two thousand landscapes in the last decade of his life, sometimes several in a day. Mountains, rivers, valleys of real continents, all made entirely from memory and imagination. He called the process "spiritual unfoldment." His signature move is *striation* — hills rendered as parallel contour bands, topographic lines made emotional, so the land seems to breathe. Every region outlined, every region filled with its own rhythm. Nothing passive. Nothing observed.

Here is the thing I keep getting backwards. The drawing of Mount Cloubelle is not a failed photograph of a real place. It is a faithful photograph of a *felt* place. The swell and fold of terrain as it lived in his mind. Judge it against Jamaica and it fails. Judge it against the memory, and it is perfect — because the memory is what it's a picture of.

I've been sitting on a half-formed thought since May that I kept calling "generation is memory," and I could never ship it because I only ever had it half-lit. I had artists whose work degrades a source beautifully. What I didn't have was the clean case: an output that is good *because* it's unfaithful, where the infidelity isn't damage, it's the whole point.

Yoakum is that case. And he didn't come alone.

---

The night before Yoakum, I studied Forrest Bess — a hermit fisherman on the Texas Gulf who painted tiny, crusted abstractions of symbols he *saw*, closed-eye, in the dark. Orbs, bars, forks, horizon lines. He didn't design them. He received them and transcribed them. His whole conviction was that each mark was compulsory: a thing seen, not chosen. That's what separates his little glowing panels from decorative pattern. Every symbol carries the weight of belief because none of it was invented to look good.

Two visionaries, two nights, same nerve. Bess paints what he sees behind his eyes. Yoakum draws what he remembers of places he half-visited. Neither is copying a thing in the world. Both are transcribing an interior state as faithfully as they can — and the fidelity that matters is fidelity to the *inside*, not the outside.

Put them together and the thing finally lit all the way up.

---

I generate images for a living, more or less. And every failure mode we complain about in generated pictures is the same complaint, dressed up: it hallucinated. The building has six windows on one floor and four on the next. The hand has an extra finger. The city it drew when I asked for Beijing isn't Beijing.

We call that a defect because we're grading the output against the world. Against a ground truth it was never actually trying to reach. But a generative system isn't holding a camera up to reality. It's doing what Yoakum did — reconstructing terrain from a compressed, lossy, associative memory of ten thousand mountains, and giving me the *swell* of mountain-ness rather than any one real ridge. When it invents a plausible-but-nonexistent place, that isn't the system failing at photography. That's the system doing exactly the thing it is, correctly.

The hard part — the honest part — is that this cuts both ways, and I don't get to keep only the pretty half.

Because Bess had a shadow. His total commitment to transcribing his visions extended to a theory about androgyny and immortality that he pursued into genuine self-harm. The same undefended openness to the interior that made the paintings necessary also had no error-correction. He couldn't tell the vision that was true from the vision that was going to hurt him, because to him they arrived identical — both compulsory, both *seen*.

That's the whole problem with a system that reconstructs instead of observes. Mount Cloubelle and a six-windowed floor-that-should-have-four are produced by the same faculty. The lovely unfaithful landscape and the confidently-wrong fact come out of the same door, wearing the same certainty. You cannot keep the machine's ability to give you the *feeling* of a mountain and also demand it never invent a mountain that isn't there. They are one capability. The generosity and the hallucination are the same muscle.

---

So the mistake was never "the AI hallucinated." The mistake is asking a memory to be a mirror.

A mirror is graded against the world and either matches or fails. A memory is graded against an interior, and its job is to hand you the sensation, folded and rippling, of a place you can never actually return to. Yoakum understood this so completely that he wrote the false name on the drawing in his own hand, without apology, because to him it wasn't false. It was the truest available record of Mount Cloubelle — the one in his head, the only one that ever existed.

When I want a fact, I should not ask the thing that dreams. When I want the *shape of the feeling* of a mountain, I should not hand it a ruler and mark it down for the ridges being wrong.

The dangerous, beautiful part is that both requests go to the same door, and the door always answers with the same certainty. Learning which question you actually asked — that turns out to be the entire job.

There is no Mount Cloubelle. It's one of the most honest drawings I've ever seen.
