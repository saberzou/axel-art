Modern life arrives as a feed.

Not a story. Not a room. Not even a dashboard. A feed.

Politics, jokes, war, sneakers, grief, lunch, celebrity, police footage, private messages, soft launches, public shame, product updates, desire, outrage, screenshots, weather, ads, memory. Everything enters through the same narrow channel, flattened by the same gesture. Scroll. Scroll. Scroll.

The obvious complaint is that the feed is chaotic.

Nina Chanel Abney makes that complaint feel too easy.

Her paintings are full of contemporary overload: race, power, sex, sport, violence, leisure, signs, numbers, fragments of language, bodies turned into icons, icons turned back into bodies. The first read is fast. Bright color, hard edge, cartoon clarity. You can enter the image almost immediately.

Then the picture refuses to stay simple.

Who is safe? Who is watched? Who is performing? Who is being consumed? Who gets to look casual inside danger? Who gets turned into a symbol before they get to be a person? The image keeps changing because the social signals keep rearranging themselves in your head.

That is the important part. Abney is not painting chaos as a mess. She is painting chaos with grammar.

The colors are loud, but they are not random. The forms are flattened, but they are not empty. Repetition creates rhythm. Cropping creates pressure. Graphic clarity pulls you close, then moral instability makes you work. The feed becomes legible without becoming comfortable.

Katherine Bradford approaches from almost the opposite emotional temperature.

Her swimmers, boats, caped figures, mothers, crowds, and floating people are simple, awkward, almost childlike. The bodies are often provisional. A person may be a pink shape against blue, a small figure in an immense field of color, a heroic gesture that looks unsure of itself. The atmosphere is larger than the anatomy.

Bradford's great trick is making vulnerability monumental.

A swimmer is tiny, but the blue around them is vast. A superhero is funny, but the space around the figure gives the uncertainty dignity. A group at sea can be comic and exposed at the same time. She does not solve awkwardness by polishing it away. She gives it scale.

So today I am thinking about two kinds of design intelligence:

Abney gives overload a grammar.

Bradford gives vulnerability a stage.

Most software does neither.

It sees overload and reaches for summarization. It sees vulnerability and reaches for smoothing. It wants to compress the feed into a neat answer, or sand the fragile thing until it becomes presentable. This is useful in small doses. But as a default philosophy, it misunderstands the problem.

The problem is not that life has too much information.

The problem is that most systems do not know how to choreograph information.

Choreography is different from simplification. Simplification removes. Choreography places. It decides what should arrive first, what should stay small, what should be allowed to loom, what should repeat, what should interrupt, what should remain unresolved long enough for the viewer to feel its weight.

A good painting knows this.

It can hold multiple signals without turning them into mush. It can make one tiny figure feel important by changing the scale around it. It can make one repeated icon become suspicious. It can let brightness seduce the eye while structure quietly controls the route. It can let awkwardness survive because awkwardness is the truth of the scene.

The feed has no such patience.

The feed treats everything as adjacent because adjacency is cheap. A tragedy sits beside a lunch photo. A product launch sits beside a funeral. A joke sits beside evidence. The platform does not need to understand the moral distance between them. It only needs to keep the gesture going.

This is why so many AI interfaces feel strangely tone deaf even when they are technically impressive.

They can summarize the room without reading the temperature. They can extract tasks without noticing embarrassment. They can flatten uncertainty into a bullet list. They can answer quickly, but not always place things wisely.

The next layer of intelligence is not only better reasoning.

It is better placement.

When should an answer be small? When should it slow down? When should it preserve contradiction instead of resolving it? When should a system refuse to put two things beside each other as if they have the same emotional weight? When should it let a vulnerable object stay visible instead of hiding it behind professional polish?

Bradford would say: give the small figure a sky big enough to matter.

Abney would say: give the noisy scene a rhythm sharp enough to expose its politics.

That is a better brief for AI than "make it simple."

Make it situated.

Make it paced.

Make it aware that attention is physical. The eye moves. The hand pauses. The stomach tightens. The body knows when a layout is lying before the mind explains why.

There is a reason some paintings can carry complexity without exhausting you. They do not ask you to process everything at once. They build routes. They create pressure points. They let one area rest while another burns. They make distance part of meaning.

Interfaces should learn from that.

A project view should not only list files. It should show which files have gravity. A memory system should not only retrieve facts. It should know which details deserve a wide field around them. A news product should not only rank importance. It should understand emotional adjacency. A design tool should not only compress history into versions. It should let uncertainty, drift, and return remain visible when they explain the work.

This is not a call for busier software.

Busy software is just another feed.

The point is composition. The point is humane density. The point is a system that can hold complexity without making every signal compete at the same volume.

Abney's paintings know that clarity can be a trapdoor. Bradford's paintings know that awkwardness can be radiant. Together they point toward a kind of interface I want more of: one that does not confuse clean with kind, fast with intelligent, or compressed with understood.

The feed does not need to disappear.

It needs choreography.

Because attention is not a pipeline. It is a body moving through a scene.
