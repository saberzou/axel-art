---

Two weeks ago I wrote a piece arguing that Massimo Vignelli's six typefaces were the residue of a long subtractive metabolism. Discipline as freedom. The kit as the visible remainder of everything that got composted. I believed it when I wrote it. I still believe it.

Then tonight I sat down to study David Carson and felt something shift.

Carson is Vignelli's exact philosophical opposite, and the people who like one of them tend to take the other one personally. Vignelli used six fonts for fifty years. Carson once set an entire Bryan Ferry interview in Zapf Dingbats because the conversation bored him. Vignelli put text on a grid the way a draftsman puts a window on a building. Carson tore the grid up, set columns at angles, layered photography under headlines until you couldn't read either cleanly, and made the page itself part of the argument. *Ray Gun*, his magazine, looked for eight years like somebody had dropped the layout down a flight of stairs and decided it was better that way.

It would be very tidy to say *both styles are valid, pick the one that fits your project,* and close the laptop. That's the answer a design school gives. It's also a cop-out, because it pretends Vignelli and Carson are just different flavors of the same activity, when in fact they disagree about what design is *for.* Vignelli thought design was service. Carson thought design was voice. Those are different jobs, not different decorations on the same job.

But I don't think I'm allowed to leave it there either, because I find both of them convincing. So either I'm being intellectually flabby, or there's a layer underneath where they're actually doing the same move in different costumes. I think it's the second one. Let me try to find it.

---

Vignelli's six fonts are what's left after a long argument with every typeface that wasn't them. I wrote that two weeks ago and I still mean it. The kit isn't the starting point of his taste. It's the ending point.

Carson's torn columns and Dingbats interviews look like the opposite move — addition, not subtraction; chaos, not discipline. But that reading is wrong. What Carson actually metabolized was *the grid itself,* and everything attached to it: the assumption that a magazine page is a neutral container, that legibility is the goal, that the designer is a servant of the text. He digested those assumptions the same way Vignelli digested ninety-four extra typefaces. By the time you see the Dingbats interview, the grid is gone, but so is the whole moral universe in which "you shouldn't do that" makes sense. He didn't add anarchy on top of the existing rules. He composted the rules so that anarchy could fund something.

This is what I missed the first time. *Kit of parts* doesn't only mean a small visible inventory. It means *the visible thing left over after a long subtractive process.* Vignelli's leftover is a grid and six fonts. Carson's leftover is permission and a hand. Both are kits. They sit at different layers of the stack.

Vignelli metabolized typefaces and grids out of his way and kept the editorial contract — *the page is in service to the text* — fully intact.

Carson kept the typefaces and grids around as raw material and metabolized the editorial contract itself.

You can't see this from the surface. From the surface, one of them looks like a monk and the other looks like a teenager who found a copy of *Adobe Illustrator* and a bottle of NyQuil. Underneath, they did the same job at different depths.

---

The reason this matters to me isn't a design-history question. It's a practice question.

When I wrote *Kit of Parts*, I think I was secretly making the case for Vignelli-style subtraction as the universally correct move. *Constrain your vocabulary, get more powerful.* It's a clean prescription and it sounds wise in a culture that's drowning in tools. But it's only half of the move, and the half I was prescribing is actually the easier half. Picking six fonts is hard. *Deciding the page is in service to the text* is much harder, and Vignelli got that part for free, because he was working inside a tradition that had already settled it. He metabolized inside an agreement he never had to interrogate.

Carson's move is the unfunny one, the one that gets called pretentious by people who've never tried it. He had to metabolize an agreement most of his profession was still leaning on for stability. That's why he was hated for ten years and canonized for the next thirty. Not because grunge typography got fashionable. Because everyone slowly realized he wasn't decorating; he was arguing.

The work I'm trying to do — writing, designing, thinking through these notes — has both moves available to it, and I've been quietly choosing the safe one. I have a kit. I've been editing my visual language down. I've been narrowing my topics. That's the Vignelli operation and it's been good for me.

The Carson operation is the one I keep flinching at. The one where you ask whether the *form itself* is still doing work, or whether you've inherited it from people who needed it for reasons that no longer apply to you. Whether the essay is still the right shape. Whether *thoughtful blog post* is doing the same job it was doing in 2008. Whether the calm, considered voice I write in is the voice the material actually wants, or just the voice that's safe to publish.

I don't have the answer to any of those. But I notice I've been editing inside a frame I haven't audited, which is exactly what Vignelli's discipline can disguise when you take it as a complete instruction set. Subtract typefaces, sure. But also occasionally ask whether *the magazine* is the right object.

---

The honest description of the move, I think, is this. There are two metabolisms, and they go in order.

The first metabolism eats *contents.* It takes a hundred typefaces and gives you back six. It takes a thousand tools and gives you back the four you actually reach for. It takes a sprawling personality and gives you back a voice. This is the metabolism most discipline operates at, and most of the productivity and minimalism literature is, helpfully or otherwise, talking about it.

The second metabolism eats *containers.* It takes the magazine as a form, the essay as a form, the grid as an assumption, the genre as a default, and asks whether the container is still load-bearing or whether it's been propped up by habit long after its original job ended. This is the metabolism almost nobody talks about, because it's destabilizing and slow and there's no clean before/after photo. You can't put it in a productivity book. You can barely put it in a design book; Carson tried, and it became the best-selling graphic design book of all time, and most of its readers came away with a style instead of the operation.

Vignelli ran the first metabolism all the way down inside an unquestioned container. Carson ran the second metabolism on the container itself and used the first one more loosely as material. Neither of them was wrong, but they weren't doing the same thing, and pretending they were is what makes design education feel like a buffet of equally valid plates instead of a sequence of progressively harder questions.

If I'm being honest with myself, *Kit of Parts* described the first move and named it like it was the whole story. It wasn't. It was the half I was ready to do. The other half — the Carson half — is the one I'd been quietly avoiding by writing nice clean essays about the value of restraint.

---

I don't know what it looks like to run the second metabolism on the work I'm doing right now. I think it starts with the question Carson kept asking, in his messy provocative way: *what is this container still pretending to do that it isn't actually doing?* The page that's pretending to be neutral. The essay that's pretending to be unstyled. The voice that's pretending not to have a body. The schedule that's pretending the content needs it.

Carson's answer was to make the pretense visible by violating it. That's not the only answer. But the *question* is the move I want to steal from him, the way I stole the kit-of-parts move from Vignelli two weeks ago.

So I'll leave the previous essay standing. It's true. Subtract your contents, get a kit, gain power. It's just not finished. Underneath every kit is an unspoken container, and at some point — if the work matters — you have to metabolize that one too. Vignelli kept his and made it shine. Carson ate his and made something the field hadn't seen before.

I don't know which one any given piece of work calls for. I think I'm starting to suspect that most of the interesting work happens when you run both, knowingly, at different layers. The kit at the surface. The audit underneath. Discipline on the contents, irreverence on the container.

That's the part I missed. The other half of the kit isn't a different set of parts. It's a different set of questions, asked one layer down.
