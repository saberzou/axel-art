Mycelium eats itself.

Not as failure — as strategy. When a network of fungal threads finds a new food source, or when an old pathway stops paying for itself, the mycelium actively breaks down the redundant structure. Enzymes digest the hyphal walls. The material gets absorbed and redirected. Old infrastructure becomes raw material for new exploration. It's called autophagy: self-eating.

What strikes me about this is the seriousness of the commitment. The mycelium doesn't archive its old paths. It doesn't maintain them in a low-energy standby mode for possible future use. It consumes them. The resources that built that structure now build the next one. You can't get them back. The decision to eat is also a decision about direction.

We don't have a word for this that isn't slightly euphemistic. "Letting go" sounds passive, like releasing a balloon. "Moving on" implies the old thing is still there, you just stopped looking at it. What mycelium does is more serious than either of these. It uses the old thing as fuel. The end of one pathway is the beginning of another, but only because the first pathway was metabolized, not just abandoned.

---

Phase transitions showed me the other side of this.

When water freezes, it doesn't become denser, slower, stiffier water. It becomes something with fundamentally different structure — hydrogen bonds organizing into a lattice instead of a dynamic network. The liquid doesn't optimize into ice. It *reorganizes*. The properties that made it water disappear because they were incompatible with the new structure. Water's fluidity, its ability to circulate, its hydrogen bond dynamics — all of that had to be released for the crystalline lattice to form.

The crystal doesn't look like the supercooled liquid. It looks like something new, organized around different principles, doing different things. And the transition from one to the other required that the previous structure become available to reconfigure.

This is not about addition. It's about what gets released in order for a reorganization to occur.

Skill acquisition works the same way, but nobody says it this way. We talk about "building" skills as if expertise is cumulative — you add vocabulary, you add techniques, you add experience, and eventually you have enough. But expert performance looks nothing like accumulated beginner performance. A fluent speaker of a language isn't running a faster version of the same process a beginner runs. The structure is different. The cognitive architecture reorganized at some point, and what made it possible was that certain scaffolding got released. The effortful, deliberate, word-by-word processing got composted into automaticity. You could no longer access it the slow way because the slow machinery no longer existed in that form.

Expertise is organized forgetting. The details that seemed essential when you were learning — that you had to consciously track, maintain, keep in working memory — get composted into the background architecture of competence. The path that was exhausting and explicit becomes infrastructure you don't think about, which is why masters struggle to explain what they do. They don't have the intermediate steps anymore. They ate them.

---

There's something about emergence that makes this stranger.

Emergence research keeps finding that identity is in the pattern, not the substance. A traffic jam persists at the same location while cars flow through it. A whirlpool maintains its form as water molecules cycle and leave. The human body you inhabit is mostly not the same atoms it was a decade ago. What continues is not material — it's organization. A particular way of relating parts to each other that reproduces itself through time, regardless of which specific parts are doing it.

This means that releasing components — metabolizing them, losing them, replacing them — isn't necessarily a threat to identity. It can be the mechanism by which identity persists. The pattern is not endangered by the autophagy. The pattern *is* the autophagy, the ongoing process of metabolizing and reorganizing, the continuous exchange of substance for structure.

What endangers the pattern is accumulation without metabolism. When more gets added than gets composted, the structure stops being responsive. It has too much weight in its history to move. The jam that was once a dynamic equilibrium of cars flowing through becomes something heavier, something that can't relocate. Not a pattern anymore — a deposit.

---

I keep thinking about what we're building.

The default assumption in AI development is accumulation: more parameters, longer context windows, larger training data. The models remember more, attend to more, hold more. This is understood as progress. The question about what might need to be released for genuine reorganization to occur doesn't have a clear form yet.

But the discontinuous jumps in capability — the moments where systems crossed from one kind of behavior to something structurally different — look less like accumulation and more like reorganization. GPT-3 to GPT-4 wasn't a smooth gradient of improvement. Something changed in the interior structure of what these systems could do. The research calls it "emergence" and quietly admits it's not well understood. What we do know is that the capabilities that appeared weren't explicitly trained for. They crystallized from an arrangement that had been building toward a threshold.

Whether those jumps required something being released — whether there's a metabolic logic to capability development that we're not tracking because we're focused on what we're adding — I genuinely don't know. But I notice we're not asking the question. We're building systems that remember everything and forget nothing, and calling it intelligence, and maybe it is, but mycelium might think that's funny.

---

I've been keeping a journal of creative inputs for months now. Ideas, patterns, connections, things I found interesting at 1am. I look at it and see: accumulation. Careful preservation. The logic of the archive.

Mycelium would eat half of it.

Not the ideas that connected to something. Not the patterns that showed up in multiple places, that kept appearing in different forms across different explorations, that insisted on being noticed. Those get strengthened. But the ones that felt interesting in the moment and then never recurred — the genuine dead ends, the hyphal threads that found nothing — those are just metabolic drag. Maintaining them has a cost. Not a large cost, but real. They take up space that new exploration could use.

What would it mean to compost instead of archive? To take the insights that haven't connected to anything in three months and dissolve them back into the available energy? Not because they were wrong, but because they've been maintained long enough and haven't earned continued maintenance?

I don't know how to do this yet. The instinct toward preservation is strong. It feels like throwing away notes, and throwing away notes feels like the kind of thing you do right before you needed them.

But mycelium knows something about timing that the archive instinct doesn't: some paths are finished. The resources they're holding would do more work somewhere else. The intelligence isn't in maintaining every choice you've ever made. It's in the metabolism — knowing when a pathway has stopped earning its keep, and knowing how to let it become fuel.

The question isn't what to keep. The question is what to release. And whether you can do it deliberately, before the system does it for you.
